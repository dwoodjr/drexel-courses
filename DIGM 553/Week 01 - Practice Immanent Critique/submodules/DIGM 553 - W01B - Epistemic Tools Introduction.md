# W01B — Epistemic Tools Introduction

> *"Instruments have a life of their own. They do not merely follow theory; often they determine theory, because instruments determine what is possible, and what is possible determines to a large extent what can be thought."*
> — Hankins & Silverman, quoted in Magnusson

---

## Tools Are Not Neutral

If [[DIGM 553 - W01A - Research-Creation as Method|W01A]]established research-creation as the methodological orientation of this course, This section (`W01B`) establishes the *epistemic status* of the tools you work with.

These two moves are connected: if making is already a mode of thinking, then the instrument you make *with* is already shaping the kinds of thought that are possible.

This is what Magnusson, following Hankins & Silverman and Don Ihde, means by **epistemic tools**: instruments are not passive, nor neutral. Instruments (artistic, musical, scientific, or otherwise) act as active participants in the construction of knowledge. 

The ***choice*** of what instrument to use is also not neutral. It conditions what questions you can ask, what results you can produce, and, eventually, what can be considered beautiful, interesting, or true.

For us, this puts our tool choices in a new light. Choosing TouchDesigner over Processing, Max/MSP over SuperCollider, a capacitive sensor over a camera are all epistemological decisions as much as they are technical ones.

## How Instruments Relate to the World (Ihde)

Don Ihde identified four phenomenological modalities of instrument-use. We will look at the three most prominent ways that instruments mediate between the body and the world:

### 1. Embodiment Relations
You act *through* the instrument, which extends your body or amplifies your senses. The instrument recedes into the background (the hidden 4th relation - ***Background Relations***).  You experience *through* and *with* technology, not *at* a technology. A well-practiced keyboard player doesn't think about the keys; they think about the music. The keys are an extension of the hand.

### 2. Hermeneutic Relations
The instrument produces data that you must *interpret*. A spectrograph, a heat map, an oscilloscope reading. The instrument mediates reality by translating it into a representation via abstractions, but that representation requires interpretive work to become knowledge.

### 3. Alterity Relations
The instrument becomes something you engage with *for its own sake*, as a “quasi-other”, a presence with its own agency. Virtual reality headsets, interactive installations, or a generative system that responds to you all produce this kind of relation.

> [!important]
> These modalities should not be considered exclusive categories. Think of them more as tendencies. Any given practice involves mixtures and oscillations between them. And they are not value-neutral: each modality shapes what you notice, what you miss, and what you expect the instrument to do for you.

## The Amplification-Reduction Problem

One of Don Ihde's most directly practical insight: every instrument involves **amplification-reduction transformations**. It amplifies certain features of the world and reduces others. And *there is no exception to this*.

One important question to always ask of a tool, technology, or model is: 
> [!question] “What is gained and what is lost when I apply it?”

- A microphone (through a purely physical model/definition) amplifies pressure-wave events in a certain frequency ranges (based on it circuit design and construction) and reduces everything else. 
- A MIDI controller amplifies discrete pitch/velocity events and reduces continuous, gestural sound. 
- A capacitive touch sensor amplifies conductivity at specific points and reduces everything that isn't electrical contact.

> [!danger] The danger is not that [tools/technologies/models/definitions] reduce. All of them do to some extent. The danger is *forgetting* that they do. When the reduction becomes invisible, the thing *not* being captured disappears from your thinking entirely.

> [!example] **Magnusson on software:**
Musical software encodes inherited defaults: equal-tempered tuning, temporal quantization, note-based event models. But they aren’t musical “facts” or “truths” and they seldom capture the full range of musical expression. What they are contingent historical choices that have been naturalized through adoption (sometimes forced) and repeated use in which what was reduced has faded away. They have become what Latour (and Bowker & Star) call **black boxes**.

## Black Boxes

A `black box` is an object or system whose internal workings have become invisible through repeated use. 
- Latour:  
  > [!quote] "objects establish themselves as black boxes through repeated use and in the process their origins disappear."
- Bowker and Star extend this: 
  > [! quote] "the more naturalized the object becomes, the more unquestioning the relationship of the community to it; the more invisible the contingent and historical circumstances of its birth, the more it sinks into the community's routinely forgotten memory."

Take note on what this means for practice: *the tools you're most fluent with are the most dangerous*. Fluency produces blindness. The instrument you've used for years is the instrument whose assumptions you've most thoroughly absorbed into your practice without noticing. 

**Critical technical Practice (CTP) connection:** This is why what Agre describes as the `"split identity"` matters. One foot in ***craft***, one foot in ***critique***. The craft foot needs fluency. The critique foot needs to keep opening the black boxes that fluency keeps closing.

## Computational Imperialism (Agre)

Philip Agre's phrase points to a specific version of “computational imperialism”: computers "aim to reinvent virtually every other site of practice in its own image." 
- They are built from representational models and abstractions of the activities they will be used to perform. 
- However, in doing so, But they also restructure those activities, fundamentally.

> [!example] Example 
> A digital audio workstation (DAW) doesn't just record and edit audio. It restructures the practice of making music around the concept of a timeline, discrete regions, channels, effects chains, automation curves, etc… This perpetuates a model of music-making built by engineers with particular assumptions about what music is.

> [!quote] A computer, then, does not simply have an instrumental use in a given site of practice; the computer is frequently *about* that site in its very design.

This is why Agre says that technical people often experience phenomenological texts as "sounding like mysticism." The mismatch in philosophy and understanding exists because the computer's representational model and the phenomenologist's model of experience are genuinely incommensurable. You can't translate one into the other because they aren't the same kind of thing. Agre's solution: stop translating, start *learning on their (meaning models and practice of phenomenological experiences) own terms*.

## The Techne || Episteme Split + Its Undoing

Thor Magnusson in ‘‘Sonic Writing” spins a particular historical framing of a driving problem this way: Western philosophy has long separated *techne* (making, craft, technique) from *episteme* (knowledge, theory, science). Instruments of art were not instruments of knowledge; skill was not wisdom.

In more modern theory and practice, this separation is now being productively challenged: 
- by cognitive science (embodied cognition, enactivism)
- by STS (Science and Technology Studies)
- by research-creation methodology
- by practitioners in new interfaces for musical expression, interactive art, and critical making.

IN doing so, our instruments become epistemic, fusing instrument with composition, as in the works of Gordon Mumma, David Tudor, and other practitioners and disciplines. The digital system is, as Magnusson puts it, `"material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control."` *All three registers are active all at once*.

## What This Means for Your Practice

Three questions to hold as you work through this course:

**What does your tool amplify?** 
- What aspects of your practice does it make easier to see, hear, measure, or manipulate?

**What does your tool reduce?** 
- What is systematically missing from what your tool captures? 
- What can't it represent
- How has the above shaped what you've been making?

**Who designed the magnification-reduction?** 
- The defaults in your software were chosen by someone with specific assumptions. What were those assumptions, and whose experience of the world did they take as universally applicable?

## Key Terms

- **Epistemic tool** — an instrument that is a knowledge-making artifact; tools determine what is possible and thus what can be thought
- **Embodiment / hermeneutic / alterity relations** — Ihde's three phenomenological modalities of instrument-world relationship
- **Amplification-reduction** — every tool amplifies certain features and reduces others; no tool is neutral (Ihde)
- **Black box** — a naturalized object whose internal contingency has become invisible through repeated use (Latour, Bowker & Star)
- **Computational imperialism** — the tendency of computing to reinvent every site of practice in its own representational image (Agre)
- **Techne / episteme** — the historically-separated domains of craft-knowledge and theoretical-knowledge, now being rejoined
