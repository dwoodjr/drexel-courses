# Agre — Critical Technical Practice

Agre describes what it's like to be both a builder *and* a critic simultaneously. He calls it a **split identity**: one foot in the work of making, one foot in ongoing critical reflection on what the making assumes. Not "make first, critique later." Both, at the same time, all the time.

---

## Why This Is Hard

The building/technical mode wants **closure**. It wants to nail down the problem, define the variables, build the system. Criticism introduces **instability**: it keeps reopening what the technical mode wants to close. So there's a real tension. Most professional settings push hard toward the technical mode: ship it, document it, move on.

Agre's argument is that the split identity isn't a luxury of academic affectation, it really cannot be. The split identity is the thing that makes practice actually good; especially in fields (like ours) where the tool choices carry such cultural, political, environmental, and epistemic weight.

---

## The Invisible Architecture

Every technical system contains a hidden layer: assumptions about who the user is, what bodies are like, what counts as normal behavior, what environments the system expects. These assumptions don't announce themselves. They're in the defaults. They're in what the error messages say and don't say. They're in what the system does when you do something unexpected.

Agre calls this **computational imperialism**: the tendency of technical systems to impose their model of the world on whoever uses them, usually without acknowledgment. The user adapts to the system. The system never adapts to the user.

Critical technical practice means noticing that layer. Making it visible. And sometimes working against it.

---

## The Practice Move

The split identity is a habit. Practically, it can look like:

- Keeping a log of moments when you hit the wall of a tool's assumptions
- Naming what you had to give up to make something work inside the tool
- Asking "who designed these defaults, and for whom?" before naturalizing them

That's what  your Critical Technical Journal (CTJ) is partly asking you to do.
