# Toward a Critical Technical Practice: Lessons Learned in Trying to Reform AI

Agre, P. E. (1997). Toward a Critical Technical Practice: Lessons Learned in Trying to Reform AI. In G. C. Bowker et al. (Eds.), *Bridging the Great Divide: Social Science, Technical Systems, and Cooperative Work*. Lawrence Erlbaum.

### Computers as Representational Artifacts

🔴 **Theory — Core claim:**
"Computers are representational artifacts, and the people who design them often start by constructing representations of the activities found in the sites where they will be used." The computer "is frequently *about* that site in its very design." It does not simply have an instrumental use — it embeds a model of the world it is meant to operate in.

🔴 **Theory — Computational imperialism:**
"Computing has been constituted as a kind of imperialism; it aims to reinvent virtually every other site of practice in its own image." Every AI system, media tool, DAW, or platform carries an embedded worldview about what activities are and how they should be organized.

🟢 **Pedagogic — Invisible worldviews:**
Students often experience tools as neutral. The computer "fades into background relation (Ihde) and becomes invisible to its many shortcomings and biases… a black box (Magnusson)." Agre's framework is the conceptual tool for making those worldviews visible.

---

### Hassles and Intermediation

🔴 **Theory — Hassles:**
"I became interested in what I called 'hassles,' which are small bits of trouble that recur frequently in routine patterns of activity." A hassle is not a one-off failure — it's a recurring friction between how a tool models the world and how the world actually works. Hassles are data.

🔵 **Project Hook — Hassle as research method:**
Agre's method: write out "in some detail both the episode itself and the larger pattern's attributes as a hassle." A daily practice of noticing, logging, and analyzing friction points in your workflow is itself a research practice. → See ASN 2 (Critical Technical Journal): structured hassle-logging *is* the assignment.

🔴 **Theory — Intermediation:**
Agre developed a vocabulary of concepts at "intermediate" levels of abstraction — neither fully phenomenological nor fully computational — that could function as the bridge between lived experience and technical specification.

🟢 **Pedagogic — Pattern recognition as technique:**
"As my intuitive understanding of the workings of everyday life evolved, I would formulate new concepts and intermediate on them, whereupon the resulting spontaneous observations would push my understanding of everyday life even further away from the concepts that I had been taught." Intermediation is a loop, not a translation.

---

### Learning on Others' Own Terms

🟢 **Pedagogic — THE lesson (Agre's breakthrough):**
"My first intellectual breakthrough came when, for reasons I do not recall, it finally occurred to me to stop translating these strange disciplinary languages into technical schemata, and instead simply to learn them on their own terms." This is how the empathize step in design *should* work — not projecting your framework onto another practice, but genuinely inhabiting its logic.

🔴 **Theory — Phenomenological texts as mysticism:**
"Many technical people have observed that phenomenological texts, when read as specifications for technical mechanisms, sound like mysticism." The problem is the reading mode, not the texts. Change the mode — stop looking for specs, start learning the vocabulary.

---

### Failure Has Intellectual Utility

🔴 **Theory — Failure as method:**
"The intellectual utility of technical exercises, as distinct from the practical utility that they might actually have in the world, lies precisely in their limitations and failures." A failed system reveals its own design assumptions. What a system *cannot* do exposes what it assumed the world was.

🟢 **Pedagogic — Even incomplete work has value:**
Projects that don't meet their creators' expectations are not abject failures — "each of them introduced technical methods that may have some lasting value." But the work can only speak with "the rigor and insight with which it struggles against the patterns of difficulty that are inherent in its design."

🔴 **Theory — Four difficulties of CTP:**
1. Becoming aware of assumptions beneath existing practices
2. Reducing a new intuition to a novel technical method
3. Preventing new methods from being absorbed back into old schemata
4. Applying new methods to real cases without reintroducing old scaffolding

---

### The Split Identity

🔴 **Theory — THE KEY LESSON:**
"A critical technical practice will, at least for the foreseeable future, require a split identity — one foot planted in the craft work of design and the other foot planted in the reflexive work of critique."

🟢 **Pedagogic — Sitting with discomfort:**
"It will require a praxis of daily work: forms of language, career strategies, and social networks that support the exploration of alternative work practices that will inevitably seem strange to insiders and outsiders alike. This strangeness will not always be comfortable, but it will be productive nonetheless."

🔵 **Project Hook — CTP as goal for digital media:**
Critical practice aims at "complex engagement, not a clean break." You don't get to stand outside computation and critique it from purity. You build and critique simultaneously. The dissertation, the sculpture, the class project — all are sites where this split identity is practiced.

---

### Entangled Oppositions

🔴 **Theory — Foucault on opposing schools:**
Via Foucault: "rather than try to adjudicate the dispute, one should investigate whether the opposed schools are internally related components of a single intellectual formation." The "computation vs. phenomenology" fight reveals that both operate inside the same formation — which is the insight that opens CTP.

## Discussion Questions

1. Identify a *hassle* in your current practice. Not a one-time frustration — a recurring pattern of friction. What does it tell you about the assumptions built into the tool causing it?
2. Agre says his breakthrough was learning phenomenology on its own terms, not as a spec. What discipline outside your own have you tried to learn that way? What happened?
3. What would "split identity" look like in your specific practice? What does the craft foot know that the critique foot doesn't, and vice versa?
4. Agre says critics are often asked "what's your alternative?" within rules that "virtually rule out alternatives from the start." How do you propose an alternative without getting absorbed back into the existing schema?
5. If tools "reinvent every site in their own image," what does your current software stack say the world is?
