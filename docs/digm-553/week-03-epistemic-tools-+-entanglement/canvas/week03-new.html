Magnusson — Epistemic Tools

Magnusson — Epistemic Tools

Every tool you use is already arguing something. It has opinions baked in, about what counts as data, what counts as sound, what a "good" output looks like. Magnusson calls these epistemic tools: instruments that don't just do things, they know things. And what they know shapes what you can think.

The Core Claim

"Instruments determine what is possible, and what is possible determines to a large extent what can be thought."

TouchDesigner thinks in networks and real-time data flow. Max/MSP thinks much the same way, but more discreetly with time via messages and patches. Unity thinks in (game) objects with update loops.

Each of those is a set of propositions about how the world is/should be organized. When you choose a tool, you're not picking a neutral vessel. You're entering a particular way of understanding what an interactive experience even is.

Amplification + Reduction

Every tool amplifies something and reduces something else. The DAW amplifies the timeline, linearity, and arrangement. This idea that music has a start and end. It reduces improvisation, emergence, the idea that music is a living event. Neither is wrong. But the reduction is always there, whether you notice it or not.

The question isn't "is my tool limiting me?" It always is. The question is: what is it limiting, and is that a problem for what I'm trying to make?

Industry Pulse — How Studios Work With This

Bluecadet builds museum and cultural experiences at scale. When they choose a platform — Unity, TouchDesigner, custom web — they're not just choosing a tool for one project. They're choosing an episteme that shapes how they interpret every brief that comes in. Their tool stack encodes what an "interactive" experience can be. Worth asking: what does Bluecadet's stack foreclose?

Meow Wolf scales experiential art into commercial spaces. Their challenge is a version of this problem too: how do you encode wonder as a repeatable, maintainable system and scale? What do their tools know about wonder that might be... wrong?

BRDG studios works smaller and more bespoke. Less infrastructure, more contingency. Their tool choices feel more situational, which means the epistemic assumptions shift project to project. Is that freedom, or is it just a different kind of constraint?

Ihde's Three Modalities (Quick Reference)

  • Embodiment relation — you act through the tool; it recedes into the background. (You don't think about the hammer, you think about the nail.)
  • Hermeneutic relation — the tool produces data you have to read and interpret. (The sensor gives you a number; you figure out what it means.)
  • Alterity relation — you engage with the tool as a quasi-other, a presence you're in dialogue with. (Somax2. The model that surprises you.)

Which one describes your current relationship to your primary tool? When does it shift?

How It Works

Pick one software tool and one hardware tool from your current practice kit (or the ones you hope/plan to use in the class).
Try to pick:

  • The one you use most.
  • The one you've stopped thinking about.

Work through the audit questions. Then: find where the two tools' worldviews conflict or compound. That's where things get interesting.

In-Class Protocol

15 min — individual audit (both tools, quietly)
15 min — pair share: what surprised you? what did the tool assume that you never examined?
15 min — full group: collect the most surprising assumptions on the board. What patterns emerge?

The Questions (short version)

What does it know?
What model of the world is this tool built on? What does it assume about inputs, outputs, users, bodies, time?

What does it amplify?
What becomes easy, fast, expressive when you use this tool?

What does it reduce?
What gets rounded off, hidden, made impossible?

Who made the defaults?
Who designed this, and for what context? Who were they imagining as the user?

Where do you hit the wall?
When does the tool resist what you're trying to do? What does the resistance feel like?

What have you stopped questioning?
What have you naturalized (made transparent or made invisible) through fluency?

Journal Entry 1 — Epistemic Tool Audit
Part of ASN 2: Critical Technical Journal, Installment 1

What This Is

The Epistemic Tool Audit (ETA) is a structured anatomy of your project's apparatus. You are going to be asking what your tools think, where you are inside the apparatus, and where the tools break (or impasse).

Building on Agre and Magnusson, this exercise rejects the idea of tools as neutral intermediaries. The hardware, software, and protocol that make up your signal chain encode worldviews, prescribe behaviors, and quietly marginalize alternative ways of knowing. This audit is one way in which we can that invisible visible.

Core inquiry: What does my tool think? Where am I inside it? Where does it break?

Step 1 — Identify Your Loop

Before you audit anything, map your primary signal chain:

[Input Hardware] → [Protocol] → [Output Software]

Document it physically (a photo) and virtually (a screenshot). This is the apparatus you're auditing, the entire whole loop.

Step 2 — The Integrative Audit Matrix

Map your loop against four research lenses. For each cell, write one analytic sentence. Don't aim for completeness — aim for honesty. Blanks are data too.

Research Domain Hardware Site Protocol Site Software Site
Sensory Worlding What does touching/holding/using this hardware feel like? What does it make visceral or signal through its form? What's the sensory reality of this connection — the clunk of a cord, the noise of a port, the heat of a running device? What does the GUI want you to notice? What aesthetic does the interface enforce before you've made a single decision?
Material Memory What embedded physics or prior assumptions are locked into this device's capsules, thresholds, or form factor? What historical ghosts live in this protocol? (OSC carries 80s hardware logic; MIDI carries keyboard-and-note-number logic.) What style presets, hard-coded standards, or defaults encode a designer's choices you've absorbed without examining?
Fugitive Speculation What is the "potential state" of this hardware — what is it capable of that it's never asked to do in your practice? Where does the handshake break down? What jitter, latency, or data loss might carry meaning rather than just noise? What latent behaviors or edge-case functions exist outside normal use? What does the tool do when you do something unexpected?
Intimate Witnessing What errors, peaks, or failures have you had with this hardware? What did those moments reveal about what the tool assumes? Where have you stayed with the trouble — kept the bad connection, the dropped packet, the lag — rather than immediately fixing it? What crashes or error messages have you ignored? Read one as a "manifesto of the machine." What is it actually saying?

Step 3 — Worldview Statement

After completing the matrix, write one paragraph answering: what does this apparatus — as a whole — believe the world is?

  • What behavior is it incentivizing?
  • Who does it want you to be: a conductor, a coder, a consumer?
  • What part of your artistic intent doesn't fit its parameters — what gets tossed to make a clean signal?

Step 4 — Deeper Audit (optional, or for the written submission)

If the matrix opens something you want to follow further, these questions can extend the work:

On worldview (Magnusson): What behavior is the tool incentivizing? Does it want me to be a conductor, a coder, or a consumer?

On marginalization (Agre): What part of my intent doesn't "fit" into the parameters of this tool? What gets discarded in the service of a clean, functional signal?

On entanglement (Barad/Loveless): Where do my habits blend into the gain-knob or the slider? Since I cannot measure something without changing it; how is my touch changing the signal?

On translation (Magnusson): What is happening when "sound" becomes "math," when gesture becomes data, when touch becomes a threshold value? What dies in the handshake? IN other words, what gets lost/reduced?

Synthesis: Where the Worldviews Meet

Once you've worked through the matrix, write a short synthesis (it could be a paragraph or a mapped diagram) on where the tools' worldviews converge, conflict, or compound:

  • Where do they align? Do they share a cosmology?
  • Where do they conflict? Do they pull in different directions?
  • Where do they compound? Do they reinforce each other's reductions in ways that are easy to miss?
  • What does the friction open up?

Prompts (to unstick yourself)

  • "The moment I realized this tool had a worldview was when..."
  • "The default that surprised me most was..."
  • "This tool was designed for someone who..."
  • "When I try to do ___, I have to fight the tool because..."
  • "These two tools agree that the world is... but I'm not sure I agree."
  • "What escapes the signal chain entirely is..."

Speculative Proposal — Template + Guide

ASN 1 Component — Due: beginning of Week 5
See full assignment brief: ASN 1: Potluck + Speculative Proposal

What This Is

A proposal in this experimental media arts lab is not meant to be seen as a list of promised deliverables. It is more so a construction of conditions. You are aiming to act with precision (pragmatism) to catalyze the unknown (speculative).

  • Manning and Massumi call this speculative pragmatism: the work of building a situation that allows something not yet known to happen.

This document is a living artifact. It will be revised (due at Week 10). The gap between the two versions (what changed and why) is itself part of the research/processs.

Format

A PDF to Blackboard. Prose, annotated diagrams, hybrid, etc.
If you wish to depart significantly from this structure, talk to me first.

The Five Components

1. Your Driving Curiosity — Finding Your "Dog"

What is the specific, situated thing that's pulling you? A relation. A question that has you in it.

  • Loveless, following Haraway, calls this your "dog," the particular thing you can't stop following, the pull that refuses to generalize away.

Focus not on what you're making but on whom and what you are touching when you engage this process.

Be specific. "I'm interested in how capacitive touch changes when the conductive surface is hair, not metal; what shifts about agency, contact, and consent" is a driving curiosity. "I'm interested in interactivity and the body" is not.

  • Try writing: "I am obsessively inquiring into the gap between and because... What I don't understand yet is..."

2. The Matter-Network (Your Assemblage)

A map — visual or written — of the tools, materials, environments, bodies, and relations that constitute your project's technical and social apparatus. Not a list, A map. One that shows how things depend on, condition, or transform each other.

Pull from your Tool Audit findings here. Locate the specific "theoretical instruments" (the audio buffers, touch thresholds, protocol choices) you're choosing to keep in your loop and why.

Include:

  • Hardware and software tools
  • Physical materials, objects, surfaces, spaces
  • Bodies (whose? doing what?)
  • Data (what kind, how captured, how stored)
  • Site or environment
  • Protocols and signal flows
  • Dependencies — what does X require Y for?
  • Unknowns — things you still need to figure out

A rough sketch is fine. The point is to see the whole apparatus.

3. Enabling Constraints

Define 3–5 specific, formal rules that will govern your making process.

  • Remember: a constraint is not a limitation; it is a catalyst for surprise.
    • They are conditions that creates productive friction without predetermining the outcome.

Constraints should be typed (as in categorized). Aim for at least one of each:

  • Technical constraint — governs your signal chain or tools (e.g., "No audio playback over 1.5 seconds.")
  • Material constraint — governs physical or environmental conditions (e.g., "Must be triggered by physical proximity only — no keyboard/mouse input.")
  • Affective constraint — governs relationship, attention, or intent (e.g., "Incorporate one error from the console as an intentional feature rather than a bug to fix.")

For each constraint, write:

  • The constraint: what it is, exactly
  • Why this one: what pressure or generativity you expect from it
  • What it forecloses: what you're giving up (a constraint that forecloses nothing isn't a constraint)

Ask yourself: are your constraints based on your skills, or are they hiding your skills? Both are valid starting points, but know which one you're doing.

4. Epistemic Positioning Statement

A paragraph (or two) on the epistemics of your project. This is where Magnusson, Agre, and Barad land in your actual work.

Try: "This tool wants me to produce ___; I will use constraint X to force it toward [a different outcome]."

Address at least two of the following:

  • What does your primary tool know — what worldview does it embed — and how is that in tension or alignment with what you're trying to make?
  • Whose body, whose context, whose way of knowing is your apparatus built for? Is that yours?
  • Where do you anticipate hitting the tool's wall — the limit of what it can think?
  • How are you entangled with your tools and materials? Where is the boundary between you, the tool, and the work genuinely unclear?

5. The Boundary Object

Loveless speaks of the "boundary object." This is a result that lives between institutional expectation and personal research identity. Both matter, and they don't always want the same thing.

Be honest about the tension:

  • What does the department/course need to see? What counts as evidence of rigor, craft, or completion in this context?
  • What does your practice / research identity need? What would it mean for this work to be true to what's actually pulling you?

These don't have to be the same thing. But naming the gap between them is useful; it helps you navigate toward something that satisfies both without collapsing into either.

Notes

On uncertainty: the proposal should contain some. If you're not uncertain about anything, you're not actually speculating, you're describing a predetermined outcome. Uncertainty is evidence the inquiry is live.

On revision: this document will be different at Week 10. Document your reasoning now so you can see the transformation. The gap between versions is part of the work.

On format departures: if prose feels wrong for how you think, talk to me. Diagrams, annotated images, recorded audio, the format should not determine the rigor.

Barad — Entanglement and Intra-action

Barad asks us to reconsider a pretty basic assumption: that things exist before their relations.

i.e.:

  • That you are one thing,
  • Your tool is another thing, and
  • Your material is yet another thing
    And then those things interact. They say no.

The boundaries between things are not given.
They are enacted through practice and relation.
This is what is meant by intra-action rather than interaction.

Interaction vs. Intra-action

Interaction assumes two pre-formed entities coming together: you + Touchdesigner (TD), each fully formed, exchanging influence.

Intra-action says: the entities emerge through the relation. The version of you that exists when you're deep in a patchwork is not the same version of you that existed before. The version of TD that exists in your practice is not the same as TD in the abstract. The practice/relation constitutes both.

Apparatus

Barad uses the word apparatus for the whole material-discursive arrangement through which knowledge gets made. An apparatus includes the social, institutional, and conceptual structures that make a particular kind of inquiry possible (through the faculties of the object). The lab, the protocol, the grant structure, the assumptions of the field, all apparatus.

For this course: your apparatus includes your hardware, your software, your studio space, your practice context, the course itself, the readings, your collaborators. That is because they are all co-constituting the phenomena you're making.

Industry Pulse — Entanglement at Scale

Bluecadet builds for institutions: museums, cultural centers, public spaces. Their work is entangled with architecture, with curators, with conservation concerns, with visitor behavior at scales from one person to thousands. The "tool" is never just software; it's a whole apparatus including the wall it is mounted on and the demographic of who walks past it.

Meow Wolf is an interesting case: they're a collective that became a company. Something changed in the apparatus. The entanglements shifted: new stakeholders, new financial structures, new obligations. What did that do to what was possible to make? This is Barad's point made very concrete.

BRDG studios works with clients communities where the entanglement is highly relational. The apparatus includes trust. It includes narrative and creative vision. It includes whether people in that room feel the purpose and story by what was built. That's its in the apparatus.

The Practical Consequence

If you are entangled with your tools, mutually constituted by them, then the tool audit is a map of the conditions of your own practice. You're not standing outside your tools observing them. You're in it. The audit is a way of trying to see something you're inside of.

That's hard. It is supposed to be hard.

Agre — Critical Technical Practice

Agre describes what it's like to be both a builder and a critic simultaneously. He calls it a split identity: one foot in the work of making, one foot in ongoing critical reflection on what the making assumes. Not "make first, critique later." Both, at the same time, all the time.

Why This Is Hard

The building/technical mode wants closure. It wants to nail down the problem, define the variables, build the system. Criticism introduces instability: it keeps reopening what the technical mode wants to close. So there's a real tension. Most professional settings push hard toward the technical mode: ship it, document it, move on.

Agre's argument is that the split identity isn't a luxury of academic affectation, it really cannot be. The split identity is the thing that makes practice actually good; especially in fields (like ours) where the tool choices carry such cultural, political, environmental, and epistemic weight.

The Invisible Architecture

Every technical system contains a hidden layer: assumptions about who the user is, what bodies are like, what counts as normal behavior, what environments the system expects. These assumptions don't announce themselves. They're in the defaults. They're in what the error messages say and don't say. They're in what the system does when you do something unexpected.

Agre calls this computational imperialism: the tendency of technical systems to impose their model of the world on whoever uses them, usually without acknowledgment. The user adapts to the system. The system never adapts to the user.

Critical technical practice means noticing that layer. Making it visible. And sometimes working against it.

The Practice Move

The split identity is a habit. Practically, it can look like:

  • Keeping a log of moments when you hit the wall of a tool's assumptions
  • Naming what you had to give up to make something work inside the tool
  • Asking "who designed these defaults, and for whom?" before naturalizing them

That's what your Critical Technical Journal (CTJ) is partly asking you to do.
