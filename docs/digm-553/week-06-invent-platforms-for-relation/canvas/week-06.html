What critique actually is

Most of us have absorbed a model of critique that functions like a trial. Someone presents work. Others evaluate it. Judgment is rendered. The work either passes or requires repair.

That model is useful in some contexts and genuinely harmful in this one.

In research-creation, critique is a different operation. Erin Manning and Brian Massumi describe the event of collective practice as something that happens between people working on a problem together, where the thinking that emerges belongs to the encounter rather than to any individual in it. Critique, in that sense, is something you do to the work with other people, where the aim is activation rather than assessment.

Activation means: opening up what is latent in the work. Finding what the project is reaching toward before it fully knows how to get there. Making visible the assumptions the maker is inside of and cannot yet see from the outside.

This is different from praise. It is also different from diagnosis. It is closer to the act of careful attention that, when turned toward something, causes it to move.

The Six Thinking Hats

Edward de Bono developed the Six Thinking Hats as a method for separating out the different modes of thinking that tend to collapse into each other when a group of people encounter a problem at once. In a standard conversation, someone might be generating ideas, someone else defending the current approach, a third person raising risks, and a fourth trying to coordinate the discussion, all simultaneously. The result is usually noisy and discombobulating.

The hats give each mode its own designated time and space. Everyone in the room wears the same hat at the same moment. You are asked to generate from a specific position, and only that position, for a defined period. Then the hat changes.

Here is what each hat does:

⚪White Hat — Facts and Information
What do we know? What is observable? What is actually present in the work as submitted? White hat thinking stays close to the surface of what is demonstrably there. It does not evaluate, speculate, or interpret. It describes.

🟡Yellow Hat — Value and Potential
What is working? Where is the energy? What is the project reaching toward that deserves to be named and developed? Yellow hat thinking is generous, but not vague. "This is interesting" is a white hat observation at best. "The constraint that the circuit closes only through body contact is doing precise relational work" is yellow hat thinking.

⚫Black Hat — Gaps and Risks
Where are the gaps? What assumptions might be shaky? What has the project left unresolved that it will need to address? Black hat thinking is rigorous, and it is also careful. The aim is to identify what the work needs, not to catalogue what is wrong with it.

🟢Green Hat — Possibilities and Alternatives
What could happen next? What directions are available that the work has yet to try? Green hat thinking generates options rather than recommendations. It opens up rather than closes down. It is speculative and sometimes impractical, and that is fine.

🔴Red Hat — Gut Response and Intuition
What is your immediate, felt reaction? What does the work do to you before you have analyzed it? Red hat thinking skips justification. You are allowed to say "something here makes me uneasy" or "there is something here I find genuinely compelling" without having to explain why. The feeling is the data.

🔵Blue Hat — Process and Coordination
What is the conversation doing? What does the discussion need? Blue hat thinking steps back from the content of the work and looks at the conversation itself. It is used to reorient, summarize, or redirect when the group needs it.

How the hats work in the room

You will not be told which hat is in use at any given moment.

What this means in practice:

  • During some parts of the session, you will be asked to generate only positive observations.
  • During others, you will be asked to identify gaps.
  • During others, you will be asked to speculate freely about where the project could go.
  • During others, you will be asked to share a felt response without defending it.

The constraint is that everyone in the room is working from the same position at the same time. If the session is in a generative phase, your job is to generate, regardless of whether you also have a critique forming. That critique will get its time.

Before next week

Next week's session is or Mid-Term Check In / Collective Critique session for the speculative proposals. You will each present your project briefly and receive a structured round of collective attention.

To prepare, spend some time this week identifying the one thing you most want the group to help you think through. This does not need to be a problem. It can be a place where you have too many options, or a tension you are holding but cannot yet resolve, or a section of the project that feels underdeveloped and you are unsure why.

Bring a version of the project that is presentable. That does not mean finished. It means legible and sharable.

You will be asked to both participate in this critique in class and also provide written feedback on a discussion forum on BB Learn. The hope is to do both of these things simultaneously, if possible.

What is happening next week

Week 7 is the first Collective Critique session. Each person will present their speculative proposal to the group and receive a structured round of attention using the Six Hats method introduced this week.

This is a studio session, which means the aim is generative. You are bringing work in progress into contact with other people who are doing adjacent work, so that each project can benefit from the thinking the group generates together.

What the presenter does

You have around ~ ten minutes to present your speculative proposal. Use that time to cover:

  • The driving curiosity: what question or relational object is the project organized around
  • The matter-network: what is in the assemblage and how it relates
  • The epistemic positioning: what the sensor or system knows, and what it has no access to
  • Where you are right now: what is working, what you are still figuring out, and the one thing you most want the group to help you think through

You do not need slides. You can bring the proposal document, a diagram, a prototype, a physical object, or whatever best shows where the project currently lives.

At the end of your presentation, state the question you want to leave the group with. Make it specific. "What do you think?" is too broad. "I am trying to decide whether the output should show the sensor's reading or the gap between the sensor's reading and the gesture: which direction do you find more interesting?" is the kind of question that gives the group something to work with.

What the audience does

Your job as an audience member is to give the kind of attention described in W06A. This means moving through different modes as the session proceeds.

Listen first. Before generating anything, let the presenter finish. Resist the impulse to form a response before the presentation is complete.

When the group moves into response mode, bring what you actually think. The session is structured, which means there will be a designated time for generous observations, a designated time for identifying gaps, a designated time for generating possibilities. Trust the structure to give each kind of thinking its turn. You do not need to fit everything into one comment.

Take brief notes. If something strikes you during the presentation that you want to hold onto for later in the session, write it down. The structured format moves through phases relatively quickly, and what feels memorable at the start can fade by the time the phase arrives.

How to prepare

Three things to do before arriving:

  1. Have a presentable version of your speculative proposal ready. This means a version you can walk someone through in ten minutes or less. It does not need to be polished or final.

  2. Identify the one question you want to bring to the group. Spend some time before the session deciding what you most need collective thinking on right now. The more specific the question, the more useful the session will be.

Making Public

Rendering work public is not the delivery of results. It is a continuation of the research — a way of making the formative conditions and processual transformation of the work available for encounter.

Loveless, working through Haraway, argues that research-creation carries an obligation to render research public — not as a finished product submitted to evaluation, but as a practice in itself. What gets made public is not just the artifact but the forces that formed it: the impasses, the enabling constraints, the entanglements, the tools and their reductions. This is why the Final Presentation in this course is not a showcase. It is a rendering.

The distinction Loveless makes is between transparency (showing your work) and accountability (taking responsibility for what your work does in the world — including what it can't do, what it excludes, and who it speaks to or past). Accountability requires knowing the stakes of the work, not just the process.

For experimental media practice, making public also means confronting the question of audience: who is this for? What does it ask of the people who encounter it? A TouchDesigner patch sent to a hard drive is not public; the same patch running in a gallery space with bodies moving through it is. The difference is not technical. It is relational — and designing that relation is itself a creative and ethical act.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 6, Week 9, Week 11

Key Questions

  • What would it mean to make your process public — not just your result? What would you have to show that you've been hiding?
  • Who is the audience for your work, and what does it ask of them? Have you designed that ask, or inherited it from the format?
  • Loveless distinguishes transparency from accountability. Where in your practice are you being transparent (showing your method) without being accountable (owning what the work does or fails to do)?
  • What form is your work made public in, and what does that form assume about who can access it?

Key Readings

  • Loveless — *Haraway's Dog* — on the obligation to render research public, curiosity as method, and the boundary object.

Failure as Method

Failure and impasse are not obstacles to the project. They are data about the project — revealing the formative conditions, hidden assumptions, and structural tensions that define what the work is actually doing.

Agre's concept of the hassle is foundational here: not a one-time crash, but a recurring friction — something that keeps happening at the same point in your workflow, for reasons you can't quite name. Hassles are worth writing down. They are the system telling you something structural about the gap between how your tool models the world and how the world actually works.

The move Agre names — and this course practices — is to treat impasse as an intellectual resource. When something fails repeatedly, don't just fix it and move on. Ask: what assumption was I making that this failure is disconfirming? What was I treating as settled that isn't? These are the questions the Critical Technical Journal is built to hold.

Manning & Massumi's concept of immanent critique is also relevant here: failure, encountered from inside the practice, reveals the pressure points of the system. You can't see those pressure points from outside. You find them by building, hitting walls, and noticing where the resistance is structural rather than incidental.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9

Key Questions

  • What's a recurring friction in your current workflow — not a one-off problem, but something that keeps coming back? What is that hassle about? What assumption does it keep bumping against?
  • When you hit a dead end in a project, how do you usually respond? Do you route around it, sit with it, or abandon it? What would it look like to study it instead?
  • What's the difference between a failure that tells you something and one that doesn't? How do you tell them apart in the moment?
  • If you had to name your most productive creative failure — the one that most changed how you work — what would you call it? What intermediation term would Agre give it?

Key Readings

  • Agre — *Critical Technical Practice* — the source of the hassle concept and the method of treating recurring friction as intellectual data.
  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — immanent critique as the practice of finding pressure points from inside the system.

Collective Practice

The studio is not a bunch of individual practices running in parallel. It's a site where practitioners condition each other.

Manning & Massumi describe the seminar or studio as an experiment: "For an 'event' to be an event, it is necessary that a collective thinking process be enacted that can give rise to new thoughts through the interaction on site." The key condition: what happens can't be predetermined. If the instructor already knows what conclusions are going to be reached and is just delivering them — that's not an event in this sense.

The critique is the most direct version of this. In this course, critique is not judgment. It's activation — attending to someone else's work closely enough to surface what it's doing that it might not know it's doing. A good critique question doesn't say "this works / this doesn't." It opens something: what is this committed to? What does it refuse? What would happen if you followed that refusal further?

There's an ethic here too. What you bring to the room — your presence, attention, honest response — is a contribution to a shared environment, not optional. This is part of how the work actually gets made.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 2, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 11

Key Questions

  • What's the difference between a critique that evaluates and one that activates? What does an activating question actually sound like?
  • What do you bring to the room that no one else does? What does the group gain from your specific presence?
  • When you encounter someone else's work, what's your first instinct — assess it, identify with it, question it? What would it mean to just attend to it first?
  • How does your practice change when it's in dialogue? What has a peer's question ever revealed to you about your own work?

Key Readings

  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — on the studio event and techniques of relation.
