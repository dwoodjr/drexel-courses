What is Research-Creation?

🔴 Theory — Prebifurcation point:
Research-creation occurs "at the constitutive level of both art practice and theoretical research, at a point before research and creation diverge into the institutional structures that capture and contain their productivity." At that point, making is already thinking-in-action, and conceptualization is a practice in its own right. The two intersect in technique.

🔴 Theory — Techniques of emergence:
"This idea of research-creation as embodying techniques of emergence takes it seriously that a creative art or design practice launches concepts-in-the-making." These concepts are mobile at the level of the techniques they continue to invent — the work is "as speculative (future-event oriented) as it is pragmatic (technique-based practice)."

🟢 Pedagogic — Process vs. product:
Manning & Massumi warn against research-creation settling "into a communication model revolving around the delivery of results." The goal is "an interpenetration of processes rather than a communication of products." This is the central caution for how to approach studio practice in this course.

🟢 Pedagogic — Openness as condition:
"The term 'research-creation' was retained as a key term for an exploratory openness in this activity of producing new modes of thought and action." R-C must be open-ended, not pre-programmed. Outcomes are "experimental, emergent effects of an ongoing process."

Immanent Critique

🔴 Theory — Definition:
"An immanent critique engages with new processes more than new products, from a constructivist angle. It seeks to energize new modes of activity, already in germ, that seem to offer a potential to escape or overspill ready-made channelings into the dominant value system. The strategy of immanent critique is to inhabit one's complicity and make it turn—in the sense in which butter 'turns' to curd."

🔴 Theory — Built-in contradiction:
Manning & Massumi note that neoliberal framing pushes research-creation toward "formatting artistic activity for more directly economic forms of delivery to stakeholders." Digital media sits in this exactly — "digital media began to take funding priority over traditional forms" partly because they "employ the same technological platforms as the increasingly computerized economy." This is an impasse, in the sense of Agre's hassle.

🟢 Pedagogic — Institutional critique is personal:
DJ's note: Drexel-style "interdisciplinary" work where disciplines "continue to work in their own institutional corners much as before, meeting only at the level of research results" is precisely what research-creation must resist. The course structure itself is an attempt to condition otherwise.

Enabling Constraints

🔴 Theory — Definition:
"An enabling constraint is positive in its dynamic effect, even though it may be limiting in its form/force narrowly considered."

🔴 Theory — Conditioning vs. framing:
"Conditioning consists in bringing co-causes into interaction, such that the participation yields something different from either acting alone… Emergent process, dedicated to the singular occurrence of the new, agitates inventively in an open field. Programmed organization, on the other hand, functions predictably in a bounded frame and lends itself to reproduction."

🔵 Project Hook — Gravity/dance example:
The jump: gravity is implaccable, but add horizontal movement and "the arc of the jump will be a collaboration between the action of gravity and the energy and angular momentum of the horizontal movement acting as co-causes." Gravity converts from limitative to enabling constraint. → What is the "gravity" in your media system? The hardware constraints, the protocol limitations, the material resistance of your chosen tools?

🟢 Pedagogic — Assignment connection:
Students will design their own "techniques of relation — devices for catalyzing and modulating interaction." These techniques must "implant opportunities for creative participation, which would be encouraged to take on their own shape, direction, and momentum." → Links directly to ASN 2 (Critical Technical Journal) and ASN 3 (Experimental Media Project).

Techniques

🔴 Theory — Two kinds of technique:

  1. Techniques to set in place propitious initial conditions
  2. Techniques to modulate the event as it moves through its phases

🔵 Project Hook — Techniques are agnostic:
"Techniques as we understand them do not depend exclusively on the content of the practices but move across their respective processes at the site of their potential multiplication." → This gives students permission to borrow techniques across disciplines.

🔴 Theory — Technique as passage:
"A technique involves activating a passage between creative forces." It involves "practiced repetition and intensive exchange." Play and experimentation extend this.

Collective Practice / Events

🟢 Pedagogic — Class as event:
"For an 'event' to be an event, it is necessary that a collective thinking process be enacted that can give rise to new thoughts through the interaction on site. It is equally important that potential for what might occur not be pre-reduced to the delivery of already-arrived-at conclusions." → Approach seminar sessions this way.

🔵 Project Hook — Co-causal thirdness:
"Each of our events seeks collectively to find modalities of experimentation that connect practices at the levels of their intensive creative force. This is done not in order to map them onto one another, or to evaluate one in terms of another, but to propose a co-causal thirdness of exploration that can be generative of new modes of practice and inquiry." → The course as a whole is this kind of event.

Research-Creation

The methodology holding critical inquiry, technical exploration, and creative production together as co-constitutive practices — not sequential phases, but registers of the same act.

Manning & Massumi locate research-creation at the prebifurcation point: the moment before thinking and making diverge into separate institutional structures. The problem research-creation names — and refuses — is the communication model: the assumption that you research first, then make something that delivers the results.

The alternative is conditioning over framing. Rather than setting up a question and then answering it, you bring co-causes into interaction and stay open to what emerges. This requires what Manning & Massumi call techniques of relation — structural devices for catalyzing interaction between forces that wouldn't otherwise meet. They are not content-specific; they transfer across disciplines and practices.

For this course, the implication is practical: the Critical Technical Journal is not documentation of a separate making process. The making is not an illustration of the thinking. They interpenetrate — or the work isn't research-creation.

Epistemic Tools

Tools are not neutral. Every instrument encodes a worldview — a set of propositions about what the world is, what can be known about it, and who that knowledge is for.

Magnusson, drawing on Hankins & Silverman and Don Ihde, argues that instruments don't merely follow theory — they often determine it, because "instruments determine what is possible, and what is possible determines to a large extent what can be thought." An epistemic tool is any instrument understood this way: as a knowledge-making artifact with its own claims built in.

Ihde identifies three modalities of how instruments mediate between body and world: embodiment relations (you act through the tool, which recedes), hermeneutic relations (the tool produces data you must interpret), and alterity relations (you engage with the tool as a quasi-other). These aren't fixed categories — any practice oscillates between them, and the shift matters.

Every tool also performs an amplification-reduction: it amplifies certain features of the world and systematically reduces others. The danger isn't reduction itself — all tools must reduce. The danger is when the reduction becomes invisible, when it gets naturalized into what Latour calls a black box: an object whose internal contingency has disappeared through repeated use. Fluency closes black boxes. The critique foot of Agre's split identity keeps reopening them.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5

Key Questions

  • What does your primary tool amplify — what becomes easy, visible, measurable? And what does it reduce — what disappears, gets rounded off, or can't be represented?
  • Who designed the defaults in your software? What assumptions about bodies, practices, and contexts are baked into those choices?
  • Which of Ihde's three modalities describes your relationship to your tool right now? When does it shift — and what triggers the shift?
  • What's the most naturalized tool in your practice — the one you've stopped questioning? What would happen if you opened that black box?

Key Readings

  • Magnusson — *Epistemic Tools* — the source text for the concept; covers Ihde's modalities, amplification-reduction, and the techne/episteme distinction.
  • Agre — *Critical Technical Practice* — on computational imperialism, the split identity, and the practice of making the invisible assumptions of tools visible.

W01A — Research-Creation as Method

"Making would already be thinking-in-action, and conceptualization a practice in its own right."
— Manning & Massumi, Thought in the Act

The Problem We're Naming

Most of what we call "research" and most of what we call "creative practice" assume they are separate activities. Research produces knowledge. Practice produces work. The knowledge informs the work. The work illustrates the knowledge. This is the institutional model — and it's the model we're going to spend the rest of this course systematically inhabiting and turning.

Research-creation is the term Manning & Massumi use for the activity that occurs at what they call the prebifurcation point — before research and creation diverge into institutional structures that evaluate them by separate criteria. At that point, making and thinking are not distinct acts. They are two registers of the same act, happening simultaneously.

What Immanent Critique Actually Means

The theme of Week 01 is "Practice Immanent Critique." Here's what that phrase means from the ground up.

An immanent critique does not stand outside a system to pass judgment. It works from inside — inhabiting the structure, understanding its logic from within, and then finding the pressure points where it might be turned. Manning & Massumi's definition:

"An immanent critique engages with new processes more than new products, from a constructivist angle. It seeks to energize new modes of activity, already in germ, that seem to offer a potential to escape or overspill ready-made channelings into the dominant value system. The strategy of immanent critique is to inhabit one's complicity and make it turn — in the sense in which butter 'turns' to curd."

Notice what this requires: inhabiting complicity. You are not exempt from the system you critique. You work within it, on its tools, in its institutions, using its grants and its grades. Immanent critique means doing that while turning it — not waiting until you're free of it to begin.

For this course: We are working at a university. We use tools (TouchDesigner, Max/MSP, Ableton, Python) that carry embedded worldviews. We receive grades. All of this is our complicity. Immanent critique is the method by which we make that complicity productive rather than just reproductive.

The Built-In Contradiction (and Why It's Not a Problem)

Manning & Massumi acknowledge a tension: the institutional framing of research-creation — especially in the context of digital media — pushes it toward "formatting artistic activity for more directly economic forms of delivery to stakeholders." Digital media gets funded in part because it "employs the same technological platforms as the increasingly computerized economy."

This is not a contradiction that resolves. It's an ongoing friction. The work is to neither ignore this friction nor be paralyzed by it — but to treat it as what Agre would call a hassle: a recurring friction that is itself data about the structure of the field.

Three Distinctions to Internalize

1. Process vs. Product

The danger Manning & Massumi identify: research-creation "settles into a communication model revolving around the delivery of results." The goal instead is what they call "an interpenetration of processes rather than a communication of products."

What does that mean in practice? When you work on a project in this course, you are not primarily building something that will be assessed. You are practicing something — developing a relationship to your tools, your materials, and your questions — and the artifact is the record of that practice, not the point of it.

2. Conditioning vs. Framing

These are technically different acts. Framing is setting up a structure: here are the rules, here are the constraints, here is the question, go. Conditioning is bringing co-causes into interaction such that something emerges that neither cause alone would produce.

"Conditioning consists in bringing co-causes into interaction, such that the participation yields something different from either acting alone… Emergent process, dedicated to the singular occurrence of the new, agitates inventively in an open field. Programmed organization, on the other hand, functions predictably in a bounded frame and lends itself to reproduction."

In this class, we are going to try to condition rather than frame — including how we structure the class sessions themselves. We set up initial conditions; we don't predetermine outcomes.

3. Enabling Constraints

The concept that connects all of this: enabling constraints.

An enabling constraint is positive in its dynamic effect even though it may be limiting in its form. The classic example: gravity is a constraint on dance, but it enables the jump. Without gravity to push against, there is no arc, no lift, no landing. The jump is a collaboration between the dancer and gravity — two co-causes whose interaction produces something neither does alone.

"In our experience, unconstrained interaction rarely yields worthwhile effects."

This applies directly to the hardware and software in this course. The MPR121 sensor constrains what counts as input. CircuitPython constrains what you can compute on the microcontroller. OSC protocol constrains the format of the data. These are not obstacles — they are the material conditions out of which something emerges. The question is not how to eliminate constraints but how to turn them from limitative to enabling.

Techniques of Relation

Manning & Massumi introduce the concept of techniques of relation: "devices for catalyzing and modulating interaction" that comprise "a domain of practice in their own right."

Techniques of relation are not content — they don't depend on what you're making. They are structural — they shape how things interact, how processes co-evolve, how different elements of a practice can be brought into productive contact. They operate in two modes:

  1. Techniques that set up initial conditions — propitious starting points for emergence
  2. Techniques that modulate an event as it moves through its phases — improvised steering without predetermined destination

A key property: "Techniques as we understand them do not depend exclusively on the content of the practices but move across their respective processes at the site of their potential multiplication." A technique is transferable. It can move from sound to movement to code to pedagogy.

Assignment connection: Part of your ASN 2 (Critical Technical Journal) practice is to identify the techniques of relation already operating in your practice — and to begin consciously designing new ones.

Opening Provocation

If every practice is already a mode of thought, what is your current practice thinking?

Manning & Massumi open Thought in the Act with: "Every practice is a mode of thought, already in the act. To dance: a thinking in movement. To paint: a thinking through color. To perceive in the everyday: a thinking of the world's varied ways of affording itself."

So: to program — a thinking in what? To route audio signals — a thinking in what? To touch a capacitive sensor — a thinking in what? To capture field recordings — a thinking in what?

These are not rhetorical questions. Your first journal entries will begin to answer them.

Key Terms

  • Research-creation — activity at the prebifurcation point where making and thinking are co-constitutive, not sequential
  • Immanent critique — inhabiting complicity and making it turn; critique from inside, not outside
  • Enabling constraint — a limitation that, through interaction with other forces, generates emergence rather than just restriction
  • Conditioning — bringing co-causes into interaction toward open, emergent outcomes (vs. framing toward predetermined ones)
  • Techniques of relation — devices for catalyzing and modulating interaction; transferable across practices

  • Agre — Critical Technical Practice (background reading; connects to W01B)

W01B — Epistemic Tools Introduction

"Instruments have a life of their own. They do not merely follow theory; often they determine theory, because instruments determine what is possible, and what is possible determines to a large extent what can be thought."
— Hankins & Silverman, quoted in Magnusson

Tools Are Not Neutral

If W01A established research-creation as the methodological orientation of this course, W01B establishes the epistemic status of the tools you work with. These two moves are connected: if making is already a mode of thinking, then the instrument you make with is already shaping the kinds of thought that are possible.

This is what Magnusson, following Hankins & Silverman and Don Ihde, means by epistemic tools: instruments are not passive implements waiting to be used — they are active participants in the construction of knowledge. The choice of instrument is not neutral. It conditions what questions you can ask, what results you can produce, and — eventually — what you consider beautiful, interesting, or true.

This puts tool choice in a new light. Choosing TouchDesigner over Processing, Max/MSP over SuperCollider, a capacitive sensor over a camera — these are not just technical decisions. They are epistemological ones.

How Instruments Relate to the World (Ihde)

Don Ihde identified three phenomenological modalities of instrument-use — three ways that instruments mediate between the body and the world:

1. Embodiment Relations

You act through the instrument, which extends your body or amplifies your senses. The instrument recedes into the background — you experience through it, not at it. A well-practiced keyboard player doesn't think about the keys; they think about the music. The keys are an extension of the hand.

2. Hermeneutic Relations

The instrument produces data that you must interpret. A spectrograph, a heat map, an oscilloscope reading. The instrument mediates reality by translating it into a representation — but that representation requires interpretive work to become knowledge.

3. Alterity Relations

The instrument becomes something you engage with for its own sake — as a quasi-other, a presence with its own agency. Virtual reality headsets, interactive installations, or a generative system that responds to you all produce this kind of relation. You are not acting through them; you are with them.

Important: These modalities are not exclusive categories — they are tendencies. Any given practice involves mixtures and oscillations between them. And they are not value-neutral: each modality shapes what you notice, what you miss, and what you expect the instrument to do for you.

The Amplification-Reduction Problem

Ihde's most directly practical insight: every instrument involves amplification-reduction transformations. It amplifies certain features of the world and reduces others. There is no exception to this.

A microphone amplifies pressure-wave events in a certain frequency range and reduces everything else. A MIDI controller amplifies discrete pitch/velocity events and reduces continuous, gestural sound. A capacitive touch sensor amplifies conductivity at specific points and reduces everything that isn't electrical contact.

The danger is not that tools reduce — they have to. The danger is forgetting that they do. When the reduction becomes invisible, the thing not being captured disappears from your thinking entirely.

Magnusson on software:
Musical software encodes inherited defaults: equal-tempered tuning, temporal quantization, note-based event models. These are not facts about music — they are contingent historical choices that have been naturalized. They have become what Latour (and Bowker & Star) call black boxes.

Black Boxes

A black box is an object or system whose internal workings have become invisible through repeated use. Latour: objects "establish themselves as black boxes through repeated use and in the process their origins disappear."

Bowker and Star extend this: "the more naturalized the object becomes, the more unquestioning the relationship of the community to it; the more invisible the contingent and historical circumstances of its birth, the more it sinks into the community's routinely forgotten memory."

Notice what this means for practice: the tools you're most fluent with are the most dangerous. Fluency produces blindness. The instrument you've used for years is the instrument whose assumptions you've most thoroughly absorbed into your practice without noticing.

CTP connection (Agre): This is why the "split identity" matters — one foot in craft, one foot in critique. The craft foot needs fluency. The critique foot needs to keep opening the black boxes that fluency keeps closing.

Computational Imperialism (Agre)

Agre's phrase names a specific version of this: computers "aim to reinvent virtually every other site of practice in its own image." They are built from representational models of the activities they will be used in. But they don't just represent — they restructure.

A digital audio workstation doesn't just record and edit audio. It restructures the practice of making music around the concept of a timeline, discrete regions, channels, effects chains, automation curves. These aren't neutral containers — they are a model of music-making built by engineers with particular assumptions about what music is.

"A computer, then, does not simply have an instrumental use in a given site of practice; the computer is frequently about that site in its very design."

This is why Agre says that technical people often experience phenomenological texts as "sounding like mysticism." The mismatch isn't in the philosophy — it's that the computer's representational model and the phenomenologist's model of experience are genuinely incommensurable. You can't translate one into the other because they aren't the same kind of thing. Agre's solution: stop translating, start learning on their own terms.

The Techne / Episteme Split — and Its Undoing

Magnusson frames the historical problem this way: Western philosophy has long separated techne (making, craft, technique) from episteme (knowledge, theory, science). Instruments of art were not instruments of knowledge; skill was not wisdom.

This separation is now being productively challenged — by cognitive science (embodied cognition, enactivism), by STS (Science and Technology Studies), by research-creation methodology, and by practitioners in new interfaces for musical expression, interactive art, and critical making.

The instruments become epistemic — fusing instrument with composition, as in the work of Gordon Mumma, David Tudor, or more recently in live-coding and instrument design. The digital system is, as Magnusson puts it, "material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control." All three registers are active at once.

What This Means for Your Practice

Three questions to hold as you work through this course:

What does your tool amplify? What aspects of your practice does it make easier to see, hear, measure, or manipulate?

What does your tool reduce? What is systematically missing from what your tool captures? What can't it represent — and how has that shaped what you've been making?

Who designed the reduction? The defaults in your software were chosen by someone with specific assumptions. What were those assumptions, and whose experience of the world did they take as universal?

These aren't rhetorical. They are the starting points for your Critical Technical Journal (ASN 2).

Key Terms

  • Epistemic tool — an instrument that is a knowledge-making artifact; tools determine what is possible and thus what can be thought
  • Embodiment / hermeneutic / alterity relations — Ihde's three phenomenological modalities of instrument-world relationship
  • Amplification-reduction — every tool amplifies certain features and reduces others; no tool is neutral (Ihde)
  • Black box — a naturalized object whose internal contingency has become invisible through repeated use (Latour, Bowker & Star)
  • Computational imperialism — the tendency of computing to reinvent every site of practice in its own representational image (Agre)
  • Techne / episteme — the historically-separated domains of craft-knowledge and theoretical-knowledge, now being rejoined

  • Magnusson — Epistemic Tools (Ch. 3, selected sections)
  • Agre — Critical Technical Practice (pp. 151–155)

W01C — Hardware and Software Inventory

"The digital system is material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control."
— Magnusson, Sonic Writing

This Is Not a Setup Guide

You could read this note as a checklist of tools we'll be using this term. But given what we've spent W01A and W01B establishing — that tools are epistemic, that instruments condition what can be thought, that amplification is always also reduction — we can't do that with a straight face.

This is an epistemic inventory. For each element of the course toolkit, we're asking: what does this tool make possible, what does it foreclose, and why did we choose it?

The Core Signal Chain

The physical-digital entanglement at the center of this course runs through a specific signal chain:

Body / Material World
        ↓
  MPR121 Sensor (capacitive touch)
        ↓
  Adafruit QT Py RP2040 (microcontroller)
        ↓
  CircuitPython (embedded code)
        ↓
  OSC / UDP (protocol over Wi-Fi)
        ↓
  TouchDesigner / Max/MSP / Ableton (host software)
        ↓
  Sound, Image, Data Output

Each arrow in this chain is a transformation — and each transformation amplifies something and reduces something. Understanding that chain, point by point, is what W01C is about.

Hardware

Adafruit QT Py RP2040

The QT Py RP2040 is a small microcontroller board built around the Raspberry Pi RP2040 chip. It runs CircuitPython — a version of Python designed for embedded systems with limited memory and no operating system.

Why this board, epistemically?

The QT Py is small enough to embed in an object, a garment, or a physical installation — it is not a "computer at a desk." This changes the relationship between the body and the computation. The computation moves toward the body rather than the body moving toward the computation. This is a design decision with consequences for what kind of physical-digital entanglement is possible.

It also has explicit constraints: limited memory, no file system (without an SD card), no display, no persistent storage across power cycles. These are enabling constraints — they push you toward thinking about computation as a real-time process happening in a physical context, not a processing pipeline running on a workstation.

Ihde's modalities here: In typical use, the microcontroller operates in a hermeneutic relation — it produces data (sensor readings) that you then interpret in software. But as your fluency develops, it can shift toward an embodiment relation, where the hardware/body interface recedes and you're thinking directly in terms of interaction rather than data.

MPR121 Capacitive Touch Sensor

The MPR121 detects electrical conductance at 12 separate electrode points. When a conductive object (a finger, a body part, water, conductive thread, foil, fruit) comes near or touches an electrode, the sensor registers a change in capacitance.

Epistemic specifics:

The MPR121 models the world as twelve discrete contact points with threshold values. It does not model pressure, velocity, texture, or gesture in any richer sense. This is a substantial reduction — and a generative constraint.

What this reduction enables: you have to build gestural richness out of combination, sequencing, timing, and creative use of what "electrode" means materially. The sensor touching a piece of copper tape embedded in a textile is not the same experience as touching a pad on a breakout board — even though the electrical signal may be identical.

The sensor does not care about the materiality of what it touches. That indifference is the limitation. Making that indifference expressive is the research-creation challenge.

Somax2 parallel: IRCAM's Somax2 system works with a similar epistemological structure at a different scale — it models musical input as sequences of symbolic events, reducing the richness of live performance to labeled categories that can be used to drive improvisation. The reduction enables the machine to respond; but the quality of the musical interaction depends entirely on how thoughtfully the reduction was designed. Same principle applies here.

Software

CircuitPython

CircuitPython is Python stripped down for embedded environments. The key constraint: no concurrency, no threads, single-threaded event loop. Your code runs top to bottom, repeatedly, in a tight main loop.

Epistemic consequence: You cannot think about this system the way you think about desktop software. There is no background thread listening for events while the foreground does something else. Everything is sequential. This forces a particular model of time — one that is actually closer to how musical performance works (step by step, no stopping) than how most software development works.

The loop structure also makes the code's relationship to time explicit. The latency between sensing and responding is determined by how much work you do in each loop iteration. You feel computation as time.

OSC (Open Sound Control) over UDP

OSC is a protocol originally designed for real-time communication between musical instruments and software. It uses the UDP networking protocol, which means messages are sent in discrete packets that can arrive out of order or not at all — the protocol prioritizes low latency over guaranteed delivery.

Epistemic framing: OSC messages have a specific structure: an address pattern (like /sensor/touch/1) and typed arguments. This structure makes the musical/physical origin of the data visible in the data itself. Unlike MIDI, which was designed around keyboard-and-note-number logic, OSC is relatively free — the address namespace is whatever you define. You are naming the world as you instrument it.

The UDP choice (vs. TCP) is itself an argument about what matters: latency matters more than reliability. For real-time physical interaction, a dropped packet is usually less harmful than a 50ms delay. This is a design philosophy embedded in the protocol.

TouchDesigner

TouchDesigner is a node-based visual programming environment developed by Derivative, used widely for real-time visual performance, interactive installations, and physical computing. It receives OSC messages, processes them in a dataflow graph, and routes the results to visual, audio, or other outputs.

Epistemic specifics:

TouchDesigner models the world as a cooking system — operators (nodes) either cook (update their output) or don't, based on whether their inputs have changed. This is a specific model of reactive computation — one that is very good for real-time visual work and less natural for sequential or text-based logic.

The node-based paradigm also has an epistemological consequence: your decisions are made visible as connections. You can see the shape of your logic. This is different from writing code, where the structure of your reasoning is embedded in syntax. In TouchDesigner, the architecture is the code.

The defaults matter here (returning to Magnusson): the built-in operators in TouchDesigner assume certain things about color, resolution, framerate, and timing that reflect the tool's origins in VVVV and cinema-influenced real-time graphics. When you use those defaults without questioning them, you're making an implicit argument about what your work should look like.

Max/MSP

Max/MSP is the older cousin of TouchDesigner — a node-based environment with deeper roots in algorithmic music composition and signal processing. Where TouchDesigner is GPU-native and optimized for visual output, Max is CPU-native and optimized for audio, MIDI, and data.

Why both? The choice between TouchDesigner and Max is not purely technical — it is partly a question of disciplinary genealogy. TouchDesigner comes from the VJ / visual performance world; Max comes from the electroacoustic music world. They carry different assumptions about what a "performance" is, what "real-time" means, and what relationship between software and body is natural.

Using both is an opportunity to feel those differences in your body as you work.

Zoom H5

The Zoom H5 is a portable, mid-range field recorder. Four inputs (two XLR combo jacks, plus the built-in capsule capsule pair), 24-bit/96kHz recording, small enough to carry anywhere.

Epistemic framing: The H5 models the world as microphone placements and headroom. Unlike software recording, field recording asks you to solve problems in space and time before you can solve them in software. You can't EQ a bad room placement. The recorder forces decisions about where you are and where the sound is in a way that a software sampler does not.

The H5 is also a tool for practice-based research: documentation, listening, locating yourself in a sonic environment. Used as a research tool (not just a production tool), it is a way of attending to a space before you decide what to make there.

The Inventory as Critical Practice

Here is the exercise this note is preparing you for:

Do your own epistemic inventory. Pick three tools you already use in your practice — any tools, not necessarily the ones listed here. For each one:

  1. What does it amplify? (What becomes easy, visible, measurable?)
  2. What does it reduce? (What disappears, becomes invisible, gets rounded off?)
  3. What world does it assume exists? (What is the implicit model of activity, interaction, body, material that its design encodes?)
  4. Who designed those assumptions, and for what context?

This exercise is the foundation of your ASN 2 (Critical Technical Journal). It begins this week.

Key Terms

  • Signal chain — the full sequence of transformations from physical world to digital output; each link is a reduction/amplification pair
  • OSC (Open Sound Control) — a real-time messaging protocol that allows physical sensors and software to communicate; address-based, free-form namespace
  • Capacitive touch — detection of electrical conductance changes; models touch as threshold-crossing events at discrete electrode points
  • Cooking system — TouchDesigner's model of reactive computation; operators update only when inputs change
  • Default assumptions — the inherited worldviews encoded in software's standard settings and built-in abstractions

Practical Examples: This Exact Stack in Research Practice

The signal chain described above isn't hypothetical — it's the system running in ongoing research on physical-digital entanglement and Afrofuturist sonic sculpture. These examples show the same epistemic questions operating at research scale, which is also what the course is pointing toward.

Example 1 — Don't Play in My Hair (Sonic Sculpture)

The setup: Capacitive touch electrodes embedded in a sculptural hair object. A QT Py RP2040 reads the MPR121, sends OSC messages over UDP to a Max/MSP patch, which triggers sound and visual responses based on which electrodes are touched and in what sequence.

The epistemic problem: The sensor models touch as 12 threshold events. But the sculpture is about touching Black hair — a gesture that carries enormous social, political, and intimate charge. The sensor cannot feel what that touch means. The gap between what the electrode reads (conductance shift at electrode 7) and what the audience experiences (touching something they were never supposed to touch) is not a technical failure. It's the piece.

What this teaches: The reduction is not a bug. Choosing what the system does and doesn't know is a design decision that carries epistemological and political weight. Agre: the computer is "about" the site in its design. This sculpture asks: what is a capacitive sensor about when the electrode is hair?

The enabling constraint: Because the MPR121 only reads 12 points, all gestural richness must be built at the audience and material level — through the choice of object, the installation context, the social meaning of the gesture. The hardware's reduction enables the cultural work.

Example 2 — OSC Schema Design as Intermediation

The setup: When building the OSC namespace for the MPR121 → TouchDesigner pipeline, you have to decide what to call the data. Options:

  • /sensor/capacitive_threshold_event/channel/7 — too technical, reveals the measurement mechanism
  • /presence_of_hand/right/index — too phenomenological, claims more than the sensor knows
  • /sensor/touch/7 — intermediate; legible to both the body and the system

The lesson: Every time you name a data stream, you are doing what Agre calls intermediation — choosing a level of abstraction that can bridge the phenomenological (what the interaction feels like) and the technical (what the hardware actually detects). Bad intermediation produces systems that are either uninterpretable (pure tech-speak) or dishonest (claiming the machine "knows" things it doesn't).

For students: Your first OSC schema is a philosophical document. What you choose to name your data channels reveals what you think the interaction is.

Example 3 — Somax2 and Machine Agency

The setup: Somax2 (IRCAM) is an AI music improvisation system. It ingests musical input — typically MIDI or audio — models it as sequences of labeled events, and generates responsive output based on learned patterns. It "listens" and "responds," but it does so by reducing live performance to labeled categories it can reason about.

The epistemic parallel: Somax2 does at the level of musical grammar what the MPR121 does at the level of touch. It models the world reductively so that the machine can act within it. The quality of the musical interaction isn't determined by how "intelligent" the model is — it's determined by how well the reduction was designed relative to the human performer's practice.

Agre's "computational improvisation" angle: Agre mentioned a system operating as "an experiment in computational improvisation; rather than constructing a plan that it then executes wholesale." Somax2 is this literally. The question it raises: what does it mean to improvise with a system that knows your patterns but not your intentions?

For students: When you build a system that responds to sensor input — in TouchDesigner, Max, or anything else — you are building a Somax2. You are deciding what the system knows and doesn't know. That decision is compositional, not just technical.

Example 4 — The Zoom H5 as Field Research Tool

The setup: Field recording before or alongside installation development. The H5 goes into a space — a barbershop, a kitchen, a corridor — and captures the sonic environment before any design decisions are made.

The epistemic function: The H5 forces you to solve problems in space and time before you can solve them in software. Bad mic placement can't be fixed with EQ. The recorder requires you to attend — to listen, to be in the space, to commit to a position. This is the embodied, pre-digital step of research-creation.

Magnusson's hermeneutic → embodiment shift: In the field, the H5 starts in an embodiment relation (you act through it, attending to what the mic hears). When you bring recordings back to the studio, it shifts to hermeneutic (you now interpret what was captured, what it means, what it missed). The shift is where research-creation happens: the difference between what the recorder heard and what you remember hearing is your first research question.

For students: What would it look like to do a listening session in the space where your project will live, before you touch any software? The H5 exercise (or any field listening practice) is the answer.

Physical-Digital Entanglement

The interweaving of physical materials, bodies, and environments with digital systems. Not an interface between two separate domains — an entanglement in which each shapes the other.

The signal chain at the center of this course — body → sensor → microcontroller → protocol → software → output — is not a pipeline that converts physical reality into digital representation. It is a series of transformations, each of which amplifies something and reduces something else. The body that touches the capacitive sensor is not the same as the threshold-crossing event the sensor registers. The OSC message is not the gesture. Something is always gained; something is always lost. What matters is knowing which something — and making that choice deliberately.

Magnusson: the digital system is "material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control." All three layers are active at once. The hardware has physical affordances that shape how you hold it, where you put it, what it can touch. The firmware has temporal properties that shape the resolution and latency of the response. The software has representational assumptions that shape what the data means. None of these layers is neutral; together they constitute the instrument.

This thread tracks how physical and digital forces meet, condition each other, and produce something neither could alone — across your hardware choices, your material selections, your installation environments, and your audience's bodies.

Key Questions

  • At each arrow in your signal chain, what is being amplified and what is being reduced? Is that tradeoff intentional — a design decision — or accidental?
  • What does your system not know about the body it's sensing? And what creative or critical work can happen in that gap?
  • Where in your practice does the physical environment shape the digital behavior — and vice versa? Can you design the entanglement rather than just inheriting it?
  • What material are you touching with your sensors or microcontrollers? What cultural, social, or political charge does that material carry — and does your system acknowledge it?

Failure as Method

Failure and impasse are not obstacles to the project. They are data about the project — revealing the formative conditions, hidden assumptions, and structural tensions that define what the work is actually doing.

Agre's concept of the hassle is foundational here: not a one-time crash, but a recurring friction — something that keeps happening at the same point in your workflow, for reasons you can't quite name. Hassles are worth writing down. They are the system telling you something structural about the gap between how your tool models the world and how the world actually works.

The move Agre names — and this course practices — is to treat impasse as an intellectual resource. When something fails repeatedly, don't just fix it and move on. Ask: what assumption was I making that this failure is disconfirming? What was I treating as settled that isn't? These are the questions the Critical Technical Journal is built to hold.

Manning & Massumi's concept of immanent critique is also relevant here: failure, encountered from inside the practice, reveals the pressure points of the system. You can't see those pressure points from outside. You find them by building, hitting walls, and noticing where the resistance is structural rather than incidental.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9

Key Questions

  • What's a recurring friction in your current workflow — not a one-off problem, but something that keeps coming back? What is that hassle about? What assumption does it keep bumping against?
  • When you hit a dead end in a project, how do you usually respond? Do you route around it, sit with it, or abandon it? What would it look like to study it instead?
  • What's the difference between a failure that tells you something and one that doesn't? How do you tell them apart in the moment?
  • If you had to name your most productive creative failure — the one that most changed how you work — what would you call it? What intermediation term would Agre give it?

Key Readings

  • Agre — *Critical Technical Practice* — the source of the hassle concept and the method of treating recurring friction as intellectual data.
  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — immanent critique as the practice of finding pressure points from inside the system.

Enabling Constraints

title: "Enabling Constraints"
type: thread
tags: [DIGM553, thread]

Enabling Constraints

A limitation that, through interaction with other forces, generates emergence rather than just restriction. The paradox at the heart of creative practice: the right constraint doesn't close things down — it opens them.

Manning & Massumi's example: gravity is an implacable constraint on dance, but it enables the jump. Add horizontal momentum, and "the arc of the jump will be a collaboration between the action of gravity and the energy and angular momentum of the horizontal movement acting as co-causes." Remove gravity and there's no arc — no lift, no landing, no jump as an event. The constraint is generative because of its resistance, not despite it.

The enabling constraint is how this course thinks about everything from protocol choices to assignment structures. The MPR121 sensor's 12-point reduction is a constraint; it enables a certain kind of sculptural thinking. OSC over UDP dropping packets is a constraint; it forces a real-time compositional response to instability. The 3-hour seminar session is a constraint; it enables a depth of engagement that 50-minute sessions preclude.

The critical move: distinguishing a constraint that merely limits from one that enables. Manning & Massumi: "Conditioning consists in bringing co-causes into interaction, such that the participation yields something different from either acting alone." An enabling constraint is always a co-cause — it only becomes enabling in relation to something else it can push against.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 8

Key Questions

  • What in your practice functions as gravity — the implacable resistance you push against? Is it enabling or merely limitative? What would it take to make it enabling?
  • What's the difference between a rule you've been given and a constraint you've designed? Have you ever designed a constraint for yourself that actually worked?
  • When you hit a wall in a project, is it usually a limitative constraint (stop here) or an enabling one (find the co-cause)? How do you tell the difference in the moment?
  • What would it mean to design the constraints of your project before you design its content?

Key Readings

  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — Propositions 0–3; the concept of enabling constraints and conditioning is developed here in full.

Agre — Critical Technical Practice

title: "Agre — Critical Technical Practice"
type: reading
tags: [DIGM553, reading]

Toward a Critical Technical Practice: Lessons Learned in Trying to Reform AI

Agre, P. E. (1997). Toward a Critical Technical Practice: Lessons Learned in Trying to Reform AI. In G. C. Bowker et al. (Eds.), Bridging the Great Divide: Social Science, Technical Systems, and Cooperative Work. Lawrence Erlbaum.

Assigned: Week 1, Week 3

Three-Dye Highlights

🔴 Red = Core theoretical concept    🔵 Blue = Project / practice hook    🟢 Green = Pedagogic moment

Computers as Representational Artifacts

🔴 Theory — Core claim:
"Computers are representational artifacts, and the people who design them often start by constructing representations of the activities found in the sites where they will be used." The computer "is frequently about that site in its very design." It does not simply have an instrumental use — it embeds a model of the world it is meant to operate in.

🔴 Theory — Computational imperialism:
"Computing has been constituted as a kind of imperialism; it aims to reinvent virtually every other site of practice in its own image." Every AI system, media tool, DAW, or platform carries an embedded worldview about what activities are and how they should be organized.

🟢 Pedagogic — Invisible worldviews:
Students often experience tools as neutral. The computer "fades into background relation (Ihde) and becomes invisible to its many shortcomings and biases… a black box (Magnusson)." Agre's framework is the conceptual tool for making those worldviews visible.

Hassles and Intermediation

🔴 Theory — Hassles:
"I became interested in what I called 'hassles,' which are small bits of trouble that recur frequently in routine patterns of activity." A hassle is not a one-off failure — it's a recurring friction between how a tool models the world and how the world actually works. Hassles are data.

🔵 Project Hook — Hassle as research method:
Agre's method: write out "in some detail both the episode itself and the larger pattern's attributes as a hassle." A daily practice of noticing, logging, and analyzing friction points in your workflow is itself a research practice. → See ASN 2 (Critical Technical Journal): structured hassle-logging is the assignment.

🔴 Theory — Intermediation:
Agre developed a vocabulary of concepts at "intermediate" levels of abstraction — neither fully phenomenological nor fully computational — that could function as the bridge between lived experience and technical specification.

🟢 Pedagogic — Pattern recognition as technique:
"As my intuitive understanding of the workings of everyday life evolved, I would formulate new concepts and intermediate on them, whereupon the resulting spontaneous observations would push my understanding of everyday life even further away from the concepts that I had been taught." Intermediation is a loop, not a translation.

Learning on Others' Own Terms

🟢 Pedagogic — THE lesson (Agre's breakthrough):
"My first intellectual breakthrough came when, for reasons I do not recall, it finally occurred to me to stop translating these strange disciplinary languages into technical schemata, and instead simply to learn them on their own terms." This is how the empathize step in design should work — not projecting your framework onto another practice, but genuinely inhabiting its logic.

🔴 Theory — Phenomenological texts as mysticism:
"Many technical people have observed that phenomenological texts, when read as specifications for technical mechanisms, sound like mysticism." The problem is the reading mode, not the texts. Change the mode — stop looking for specs, start learning the vocabulary.

Failure Has Intellectual Utility

🔴 Theory — Failure as method:
"The intellectual utility of technical exercises, as distinct from the practical utility that they might actually have in the world, lies precisely in their limitations and failures." A failed system reveals its own design assumptions. What a system cannot do exposes what it assumed the world was.

🟢 Pedagogic — Even incomplete work has value:
Projects that don't meet their creators' expectations are not abject failures — "each of them introduced technical methods that may have some lasting value." But the work can only speak with "the rigor and insight with which it struggles against the patterns of difficulty that are inherent in its design."

🔴 Theory — Four difficulties of CTP:

  1. Becoming aware of assumptions beneath existing practices
  2. Reducing a new intuition to a novel technical method
  3. Preventing new methods from being absorbed back into old schemata
  4. Applying new methods to real cases without reintroducing old scaffolding

The Split Identity

🔴 Theory — THE KEY LESSON:
"A critical technical practice will, at least for the foreseeable future, require a split identity — one foot planted in the craft work of design and the other foot planted in the reflexive work of critique."

🟢 Pedagogic — Sitting with discomfort:
"It will require a praxis of daily work: forms of language, career strategies, and social networks that support the exploration of alternative work practices that will inevitably seem strange to insiders and outsiders alike. This strangeness will not always be comfortable, but it will be productive nonetheless."

🔵 Project Hook — CTP as goal for digital media:
Critical practice aims at "complex engagement, not a clean break." You don't get to stand outside computation and critique it from purity. You build and critique simultaneously. The dissertation, the sculpture, the class project — all are sites where this split identity is practiced.

Entangled Oppositions

🔴 Theory — Foucault on opposing schools:
Via Foucault: "rather than try to adjudicate the dispute, one should investigate whether the opposed schools are internally related components of a single intellectual formation." The "computation vs. phenomenology" fight reveals that both operate inside the same formation — which is the insight that opens CTP.

Discussion Questions

  1. Identify a hassle in your current practice. Not a one-time frustration — a recurring pattern of friction. What does it tell you about the assumptions built into the tool causing it?
  2. Agre says his breakthrough was learning phenomenology on its own terms, not as a spec. What discipline outside your own have you tried to learn that way? What happened?
  3. What would "split identity" look like in your specific practice? What does the craft foot know that the critique foot doesn't, and vice versa?
  4. Agre says critics are often asked "what's your alternative?" within rules that "virtually rule out alternatives from the start." How do you propose an alternative without getting absorbed back into the existing schema?
  5. If tools "reinvent every site in their own image," what does your current software stack say the world is?

Epistemic Tools

Magnusson, T. (2019). 'Epistemic Tools.' In Sonic Writing: Technologies of Material, Symbolic, and Signal Inscriptions. Bloomsbury Academic. [Ch. 3, selected sections]

Assigned: Week 1, Week 2

Three-Dye Highlights

🔴 Red = Core theoretical concept    🔵 Blue = Project / practice hook    🟢 Green = Pedagogic moment

Tools Determine What Is Possible

🔴 Theory — Foundational claim (Hankins & Silverman):
"Instruments have a life of their own. They do not merely follow theory; often they determine theory, because instruments determine what is possible, and what is possible determines to a large extent what can be thought." This is the core argument for the concept of epistemic tools — the tool is not neutral; it is an epistemic artifact, carrying a worldview into every act of use.

🟢 Pedagogic — Course hardware rationale:
Why do we use specific hardware in this course rather than staying entirely in software? Because the choice of instrument conditions what kinds of thinking and making are possible. The MPR121 capacitive touch sensor does not "just" detect touch — it frames touch as a particular kind of data. The Zoom H5 does not "just" record — it frames sound as a particular kind of capture.

Ihde's Phenomenological Modalities

🔴 Theory — Three modalities of instrument relation (Ihde 1990):

  • Embodiment relations: the instrument extends the body or amplifies the senses (we act through it)
  • Hermeneutic relations: the instrument provides data we must interpret — like a spectrograph or heat map
  • Alterity relations: the technology becomes something we engage with for its own sake — virtual reality, for instance, or a musical instrument that plays back

🔵 Project Hook — Entangled modalities:
These relations are "not in isolation from one another, but are themselves an entangled web/knot." A single session working with TouchDesigner and a sensor involves all three simultaneously — you act through it (embodiment), read its outputs (hermeneutic), and sometimes play with it as its own thing (alterity). The task is to notice which mode is active and when.

Amplification-Reduction Transformations

🔴 Theory — No neutral tool (Ihde 1979):
"Technology equally conceals and reveals… Technologies organise, select, focus the task, and shape the environment through various transformations. This is what Ihde calls amplification-reduction transformations." Every tool amplifies certain features of the world and reduces others. There is no tool that is neutral.

🔵 Project Hook — Inherited defaults as worldviews:
Musical software carries embedded assumptions: "halftone equal tempered tuning, temporal quantisation, or note-based event model." These are not inevitable features of music — they are contingent historical choices now naturalized into default. → What defaults has your current software stack inherited? Which do you accept, which do you resist?

🟢 Pedagogic — Tool critique is practice critique:
"We must become critically aware of, navigate, and refute (become fugitives) when we pick and utilize certain tools. We must not subconsciously and unknowingly continue to present certain worldviews if they are not aligned with our being." This is not about avoiding tools — it is about practicing with a critical awareness of what they bring.

Black Boxes

🔴 Theory — Naturalization (Latour / Bowker & Star):
"Objects establish themselves as black boxes through repeated use and in the process their origins disappear. The object is naturalised through heavy use, a fact prompting Bowker and Star to observe that the 'more naturalized the object becomes, the more unquestioning the relationship of the community to it; the more invisible the contingent and historical circumstances of its birth, the more it sinks into the community's routinely forgotten memory.'"

🟢 Pedagogic — Black-boxing as danger:
The danger is not complexity — it's forgetting. When we forget that a tool is a contingent artifact, we stop questioning it. CTP (Agre) and research-creation (Manning & Massumi) are both, in part, practices of deliberately opening black boxes back up.

Techne / Episteme

🔴 Theory — The separation and its questioning:
"If the problem of Western philosophy has been the separation of techne and episteme, this dichotomy is now being questioned, following a better understanding in cognitive science and the role of active perception." The early modern conception: instruments are "containers of theory" — the musician engages with a system of thought when performing on an instrument.

🔴 Theory — Three modes of epistemological work (Baird):

  1. Model knowledge: the instrument as material mode of representation
  2. Working knowledge: the instrument as mode of effective action
  3. Encapsulated knowledge: the instrument synthesizing representation and action together

"Technological artefacts are models of thought, performing epistemological work alongside scientific theories, and often preceding them."

🟢 Pedagogic — The historical question:
When did instruments of the arts lose their epistemic status? When did the art instrument cease to be studied as a knowledge-making device? The interdisciplinary turn is, in part, a recovery of that status.

The Digital System

🔴 Theory — Material / electric / symbolic:
"The digital system is material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control." Unlike acoustic instruments, where most time with the instrument does not involve symbolic thought, digital instruments are fundamentally representational. "Behind our simple instrument are layers of microchips, busses, memory; operating systems, code libraries, classes; protocols, standards."

🔵 Project Hook — Designer decides:
"The designer decides what is revealed and what is concealed when the system is used." When the designer is also the performer, the researcher, and the observer — as in autoethnographic research practice — navigating these roles requires deliberate switching.

Human-Machine Play

🔵 Project Hook — Play as modality:
"In music technologies, this manifests through play, through playing the instrument, but also the playful interaction that can emerge between a human and machine if the latter is given agency." The question of machine agency — explored in Somax2, in generative systems, in AI improvisation — is precisely the question of what kind of play becomes possible when the instrument pushes back.

Discussion Questions

  1. Pick one tool you use daily. What does it amplify about your practice? What does it reduce or make invisible?
  2. Magnusson says the digital system is "material in its body, electric in function, symbolic in control." What does the "symbolic" layer of your tool hide about the material layer underneath?
  3. Ihde describes embodiment, hermeneutic, and alterity relations with instruments. Which of these dominates your current hardware/software setup, and why?
  4. What in your current stack is a black box you've stopped questioning? What would happen if you opened it?
  5. Hankins & Silverman say tools determine theory. What theory is being determined by the specific hardware we're using in this course?
