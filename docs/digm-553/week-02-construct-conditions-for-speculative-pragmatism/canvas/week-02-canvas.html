Collective Practice

Collective Practice

The studio is not a bunch of individual practices running in parallel. It's a site where practitioners condition each other.

Manning & Massumi describe the seminar or studio as an experiment: "For an 'event' to be an event, it is necessary that a collective thinking process be enacted that can give rise to new thoughts through the interaction on site." The key condition: what happens can't be predetermined. If the instructor already knows what conclusions are going to be reached and is just delivering them — that's not an event in this sense.

The critique is the most direct version of this. In this course, critique is not judgment. It's activation — attending to someone else's work closely enough to surface what it's doing that it might not know it's doing. A good critique question doesn't say "this works / this doesn't." It opens something: what is this committed to? What does it refuse? What would happen if you followed that refusal further?

There's an ethic here too. What you bring to the room — your presence, attention, honest response — is a contribution to a shared environment, not optional. This is part of how the work actually gets made.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 2, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 11

Key Questions

  • What's the difference between a critique that evaluates and one that activates? What does an activating question actually sound like?
  • What do you bring to the room that no one else does? What does the group gain from your specific presence?
  • When you encounter someone else's work, what's your first instinct — assess it, identify with it, question it? What would it mean to just attend to it first?
  • How does your practice change when it's in dialogue? What has a peer's question ever revealed to you about your own work?

  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — on the studio event and techniques of relation.

Physical-Digital Entanglement

Physical and digital aren't two separate domains with an interface between them. They're entangled — each one shaping the other.

The signal chain at the center of this course — body → sensor → microcontroller → protocol → software → output — is not a pipeline that converts physical reality into digital representation. It's a series of transformations. At each arrow, something is amplified and something is reduced. The body that touches a capacitive sensor is not the same as the threshold-crossing event the sensor registers. The OSC message is not the gesture. Something is always gained; something is always lost. The question is whether you know which — and whether that choice is deliberate.

Magnusson puts it directly: the digital system is "material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control." All three layers are active at once. The hardware has physical affordances that shape where you can put it and what it can touch. The firmware has timing properties that shape resolution and latency. The software has representational assumptions that shape what the data means. None of these layers is neutral; together they constitute the instrument.

This thread tracks how physical and digital forces meet, condition each other, and produce something neither could alone — across hardware choices, material selections, installation environments, and bodies moving through space.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 10

Key Questions

  • At each arrow in your signal chain, what's being amplified and what's being reduced? Is that tradeoff intentional?
  • What does your system not know about the body it's sensing — and what can happen in that gap?
  • Where does the physical environment shape the digital behavior, and vice versa? Can you design the entanglement rather than just inheriting it?
  • What material are your sensors touching? What cultural or social charge does that material carry — and does your system acknowledge it?

Key Readings

  • Magnusson — *Epistemic Tools* — on instruments as material-electric-symbolic systems.
  • Camus + Vinck — *Unfolding Digital Materiality* — on the thickness and materiality of digital objects.

Enabling Constraints

A constraint that opens things up rather than just closing them down.

Manning & Massumi's example: gravity. You can't dance without gravity — remove it and there's no jump, no landing, no arc. Gravity is implacable, but it's also what makes the jump possible. Add horizontal momentum and "the arc of the jump will be a collaboration between the action of gravity and the energy and angular momentum of the horizontal movement acting as co-causes." That's an enabling constraint in action — resistance that generates something rather than just blocking it.

The key distinction is between limitative constraints (dead ends) and enabling ones (co-causes). A limitative constraint just says stop. An enabling constraint pushes back in a way that makes something happen that wouldn't otherwise. The trick is figuring out which you're dealing with — and sometimes, how to convert one into the other.

This shows up everywhere in the course. The MPR121 has 12 channels — that's a constraint, but it's also the specific sculptural logic of the thing. OSC over UDP drops packets — that's a constraint, but it forces real-time compositional decisions you wouldn't otherwise have to make. The 3-hour session is a constraint; it's also what allows the kind of depth you can't get in 50 minutes.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 8

Key Questions

  • What's the gravity in your practice — the resistance you can't remove? Is it enabling or just limitative? What would it take to convert it?
  • What's the difference between a constraint someone handed you and one you designed yourself?
  • When you hit a wall, is it a dead end or a co-cause waiting to be found?
  • What if you designed your constraints before you designed the content?

Key Readings

  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — Propositions 0–3.

Research-Creation

Making and thinking as the same act — not phases, not sequence, not illustration.

The standard model: you research something, then you make something that shows what you found. Research-creation refuses that split. Manning & Massumi locate it at what they call the prebifurcation point — the moment before making and thinking get separated into different institutional categories with different evaluation criteria. At that point, making is thinking, and thinking is already a practice.

The thing this rules out is the communication model — the idea that your creative work is "delivering" research results you already had. If the project knows its outcome before it starts, it's not research-creation. The work has to be genuinely open to something it couldn't have predicted.

What that actually requires in practice: conditioning rather than framing. Framing means you set up a structure and the work fills it in. Conditioning means you bring things into contact — materials, tools, questions, bodies, sensors — and stay open to what emerges from their interaction. Neither you nor any individual element fully determines the outcome.

For this course: the journal is not documentation of a separate making process. The making is not an illustration of the thinking. They interpenetrate, or it's not research-creation.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 10, Week 11

Key Questions

  • Where in your workflow do making and thinking actually happen at the same time? Can you work from that moment deliberately?
  • Is your project conditioned or framed? What are you holding open, and what are you predetermining?
  • If your practice is already "thinking something" — what is it thinking right now?
  • What in your institution (program, discipline, grading rubric) separates your thinking from your making? How do you work inside that without just reproducing it?

Key Readings

  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — Propositions 0–3.
  • Loveless — *Haraway's Dog* — on curiosity as a research method; following a question rather than testing a hypothesis.

The Three Questions

For each contribution we're working through the same three questions:

1. What does it resist? What does it yield to?
Not what it is, but what it does.

  • What happens when you push on it?

2. What does it already know?
Every material, tool, or dataset carries embedded logic: assumptions about what it's for, what it can sense, what it makes legible.

  • What worldview is already in the thing you brought?

3. What would it take to put this in a signal chain?
What sensor, protocol, or system could turn its behavior into data? What gets amplified? What gets lost?

Instructor's Contribution — A Guided Example

I brought three things together…

1. Zoom H5 Studio Field Recorder

What it is: A portable audio recorder with interchangeable capsule system (XY stereo capsule default) + 2 additional inputs for external mics or line sources.

What it resists: It's designed to capture sound above water, through air.

  • The diaphragm is optimized for atmospheric pressure and airborne sound waves. Submerge it and it fails. That failure is a constraint.

What it yields to: Everything in its polar pattern, within its frequency response curve. It's extraordinarily accurate and faithful. Which means it also captures everything you didn't intend (room noise, handling noise, etc.). That is another of its constraints.

What it knows: That sound is something that moves through air, that has amplitude and frequency, that can be captured as a voltage signal.

  • It doesn't know about pressure differentials in water, or the acoustic signature of dissolution, or what it means for a material to "sound like" something, culturally speaking.

Signal chain position: Capture device → digital audio file → DAW or Max/MSP → granular synthesis or convolution. The H5 is the first transduction point.

2. DIY Hydrophone

What it is: A piezoelectric contact microphone sealed in an epoxy housing — built to go into water.

  • Where the H5 fails, this picks up at that moment.

What it resists: Atmospheric sound (it's designed to be submerged). High-frequency detail in air is lost. It is more “optimized” for the acoustic properties of water, which transmit certain frequencies differently than air. It also resists damage from submersion that would destroy a conventional diaphragm mic.

What it yields to: Pressure waves propagating through liquid (including the sounds that materials make as they dissolve, swell, crack, and settle).

  • The crackle of a substrate fracturing under water.
  • The CO₂ fizz of an effervescent reaction.

What it knows: That sound can travel through a medium that isn't air. That water has its own acoustic logic. That a piezo element converts mechanical pressure directly to voltage.

Signal chain position: Hydrophone → H5 mic input → digital audio → Max/RNBO granular patch.

  • The hydrophone acts as the second transduction: physical pressure → electrical signal.

3. DIY Water Dissolution Substrate

What it is: A bath bomb-style compressed “sphere” (more like loose moon-sand right now) made from baking soda, citric acid, cornstarch, clay (bentonite or kaolin), salts, and iron oxide pigment.

  • Designed to dissolve in water over 10–40 minutes, producing acoustic, visual, and chemical events as it does.

What it resists: Dissolution is its enabling constraint and its generative condition. The sphere is structurally stable in dry conditions; contact with water is the co-cause that activates its temporal properties.

What it yields to: Water. Heat. Time. Each material layer artifact or suspends at a different rate.

  • The iron oxide creates ink-like plumes
  • The bentonite swells before dispersing, clouding the water
  • The salt crystals fracture and dissolve with audible cracks

What it knows (by design): The sphere is built from materials chosen for both their cultural and sonic resonances (obsidian black, earth tones, water-soluble minerals).

  • For this, chemistry becomes a form of argument-in-action. The dissolution profile is activates a score.

Signal chain position: Sphere in water → hydrophone captures acoustic events → H5 records → granular synthesis in Max/RNBO maps dissolution data to sound.

  • The sphere is another instrument and the water the performance space.

The Guiding Question for My Contribution

What does a material know; and what becomes audible when we put it in a signal chain?

  • The H5 is designed for air.
  • The hydrophone is designed for water.
  • The sphere is designed to dissolve.

The enabling constraint of these instruments arises from their combination. The three co-causes that produce something none of them could alone.

W02B — Enabling Constraints Workshop

A limitation that, through interaction with other forces, generates emergence rather than just restriction.

The goal today is to do our best to feel and operate an enabling constraint. We're going to work with what we brought and see what happens when we push on its limits.

Connected Threads

The Gravity Example

For Manning & Massumi: gravity is implacable. You can't dance without it. You can jump without it. There are many aspects of our daily lives and activates that would be impossible without it. Gravity thus, enables the possible.

Now, take the jump. Add horizontal momentum and you get an arc: a collaboration between two co-causes. Neither gravity nor momentum alone makes the jump. The constraint is generative because of its resistance.

The question this opens:

  • What's the gravity in your object?
  • What does it resist that you could push against?

Workshop Structure

Round 1 — Describe Without Defaulting (15 min)

Place your object on the shared surface. Describe it — but you cannot use the words "material" or "art."

Instead, try:

  • What does it resist? What does it yield to?
  • What does it amplify? What does it reduce?
  • What can not be done with it? (That's your starting constraint.)
  • What does it do to the thing closest to it?

Round 2 — Convert a Limitative Constraint (10 min)

Pick one thing your object cannot do. One real limitation.

Now: what second thing (a co-cause) could convert that limitation into generative potential?

The co-cause can be:

  • Another object or material
  • A rule or instruction
  • A body position or gesture
  • A sensor or signal chain
  • A time constraint

Write down the pair: [limitation] + [co-cause] → [what becomes possible]

You're designing an enabling constraint.

Round 3 — Put It in the Signal Chain (10 min)

Pick one object from the table. [Conceptually] connect your object to a sensor. (or if you have the stuff, you could actually do it)

At the moment of connection: pause. Ask:

  • What just happened to this object? What did it become?
  • What does the sensor now "know" about the object? What doesn't it know?
  • What's being amplified? What's being reduced?

Write 2–3 sentences: what did the object become when it entered the signal chain?

This is the physical-digital entanglement moment — a foreign object enters a circuit and both things change.

Round 4 — Second Descriptions (10 min) [Optional]

Return to your original object. Describe it again — same no-"material"/no-"art" constraint from Round 1, but now you've been through the workshop.

Did your description change? If yes: what changed it? If no: what does that tell you about how you're relating to this object?

Group discussion: what's the enabling constraint of the workshop itself?

Examples — Works That Use Constraints as Generative Logic

A few works where the constraint is the piece, not an obstacle to it:

Alvin Lucier — I Am Sitting in a Room (1969)
The room's acoustic resonance is the constraint. Lucier records himself speaking, plays it back into the room, records that, plays it back again, until the room's frequencies overtake his voice entirely. The limitation (imperfect playback in a reverberant space) becomes the compositional process.
→ Listen / read

Neri Oxman — Material Ecology / Silk Pavilion (2013)
The constraint is the silkworm's biology: it can only spin within certain temperature and humidity ranges, following pheromone traces. Oxman designs the armature; the worms design the surface. The biological constraint shapes the architecture.
→ MIT Media Lab documentation

Tarek Atoui — The Reverse Collection (2016)
Atoui works with Deaf and hearing-impaired communities to design instruments that bypass conventional acoustic listening. The "constraint" of non-standard hearing becomes the condition for new instrument forms.
→ Frieze profile

Pauline Oliveros — Deep Listening practice
Not a single work, but a practice built around the constraint of duration and attention: listening so slowly and deeply that normally unnoticed sounds become material. The constraint is temporal: you are not allowed to move on.
→ Deep Listening Institute

Torkwase Dyson — Black Compositional Thought (ongoing)
Dyson uses the constraint of a fixed geometric vocabulary — arcs, rectangles, horizontal planes — to think spatial liberation for Black bodies. The limitation of the vocabulary becomes the condition for specificity.
→ Artist site

Add your own examples here as the course develops.
