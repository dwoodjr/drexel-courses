Primary Readings

Magnusson, T. — Sonic Writing: "Epistemic Tools"

Chapter 3 (selected sections)
Bloomsbury Academic, 2019.

Access: Magnusson — Epistemic Tools PDF

Agre, P. E. — "Toward a Critical Technical Practice"

In Bridging the Great Divide: Social Science, Technical Systems, and Cooperative Work. Lawrence Erlbaum, 1997.

Access: Agre — Critical Technical Practice PDF

How to Read Them

These two texts work well together — Magnusson gives you the vocabulary for thinking about tools as epistemic artifacts, and Agre gives you the practice methodology for actually working inside that critique.

For Magnusson: He's making a philosophical argument about instruments — that they are not neutral, that they determine what kinds of thinking are possible, and that every tool carries a worldview. Read it looking for the moments that describe your own tools. The amplification-reduction idea is the core — look for where he explains it and think immediately of a tool in your own stack.

For Agre: This one is more personal — he's writing about his own attempt to reform AI from inside, and the difficulties he ran into. The concept of hassles is the most immediately practical thing in the text. A hassle is a recurring friction in a workflow that reveals a mismatch between how a tool models the world and how the world actually is. Hassles are data.

Reading Questions

Magnusson:

  1. Pick one tool you use regularly (hardware or software). What does it amplify about your practice? What does it reduce or make invisible?

  2. Magnusson says tools can become "black boxes" — things we stop questioning because they've been naturalized by use. What in your current stack have you stopped questioning? What would happen if you opened it back up?

  3. He describes three modes of relation to instruments: embodiment (you act through it), hermeneutic (you read its output), alterity (you play with it as its own thing). Which of these dominates how you use your primary tool — and does that shift depending on what you're doing?

Agre:

  1. Identify one hassle in your current practice — a recurring friction, not a one-time frustration. What does it tell you about the assumptions built into the tool (or system, or workflow) causing it?

  2. Agre describes a "split identity" — one foot in craft, one foot in critique. What does that split feel like in your own work? Is there a tension between the two, or have you found a way to hold them together?

Coming to Class Prepared

You need to:

  • Have read both texts at least once (a partial read beats a no-read)
  • Have one concrete example ready — a tool, a hassle, or a moment where you felt a tool shaping what you could think
  • Know which ideas land for you and which ones you're skeptical of — both are useful
