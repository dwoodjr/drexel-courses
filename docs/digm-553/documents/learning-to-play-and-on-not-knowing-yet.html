On Not Knowing Yet

When a person sits down at a piano for the first time and plays a wrong note, nothing bad happens. They play it again. They adjust. It is as simple as that.
Nobody grades the wrong note. Nobody expects the right one yet; especially not in the very beginning. In fact, the whole situation of learning an instrument, any instrument, is predicated on the idea that you will sound bad before you sound good

There is no “problem to solve” here, just a process to move through. The learning happens inside the fumbling, not after it.

Experimental media work has the same structure. It just doesn't always feel that way. Especially in an institutional/academic setting.

Many students enter technical and creative work carrying an invisible question: am I doing this right? That question is understandable. However, it is also almost always the least useful one to ask yourself. The institutional frame that produces it (grades, evaluation, correct answers) is useful in some contexts and actively counterproductive in most others. When working experimentally, “right” or “correct” is not a fixed target. You're trying to figure out what you are doing, and what comes next.

The shift worth making, as early as possible, is to go from performing competence, instead, to practicing curiosity. Performing competence means managing how your work looks to others. Practicing curiosity means following what interests you, especially when you don't know where it leads.

What instrument learning can teach us

A musician learning a new piece doesn't start by playing it through. They start with a single phrase or note. They play it slowly, faster than they can play it correctly, then slowly again. They loop the hard part until it stops being difficult. They listen to what they're producing, not just whether it “matches” the score, but what it actually sounds like, what it feels like, how it sounds in the room. Then they repeat. They make the same mistake many, many, many… many times.

Notice the mistake. Then adjust.

This is the method.

Working with sensors, code, and physical materials asks for the same thing. You don't understand a sensor by reading about it and then deploying it. You understand it by running it, printing its raw values, moving your hand over it, covering it, changing the light, changing its conditions, then watching what changes and what doesn't. You repeat. You adjust. You see (or hear) what it's giving you before you decide what to do with it.

The sensor doesn't know your deadline or what grade you “need.” It just responds to the physical world, and your job, right now, is to learn how.

Trial and error and failure as method

In experimental work, trial and error is the primary method.

There's a meaningful difference between random trial and error and structured trial and error.

Random trial and error: change several things at once, hope something works, have no idea what fixed it or why.

Structured trial and error: change one thing, observe what happens, draw a conclusion, repeat. This is slow in the moment and fast overall, because every iteration teaches you something you can use.

The habit to build is simple: when something doesn't work, change one thing at a time. Then look at what changed. The goal of each experiment is not to fix the problem. the goal is to learn something specific about the system. The fix will follow the learning.

This is also how you build intuition. Intuition is practice: pattern recognition built from many structured observations. The person who "just knows" what's wrong with a circuit or a piece of code got there by breaking things systematically, many times over, and paying attention.

On not knowing

Not knowing is the starting condition for all learning. In experimental work it never fully goes away; you just get more comfortable with it.

The practitioners you admire, the ones whose work seems fluent and confident, got there by being confused many times and continuing anyway. They built a tolerance for not knowing that eventually started to feel like curiosity. When that happens, confusion becomes interesting rather than threatening.

That shift doesn't happen all at once. But it starts with recognizing that what is actually happening here? is usually a more useful question than am I doing this right? One opens things up; the other closes them down.

You are learning to play. The wrong notes are part of it.
