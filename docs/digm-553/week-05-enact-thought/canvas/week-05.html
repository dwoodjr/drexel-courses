From the Reading — Key Moves in Camus & Vinck

Becoming digital. Moving something into a digital system creates a new object with different properties. The original and the digital version have different properties. Something is gained; something is lost. They are two different things.

Digital objects have thickness. They break. They resist. They require maintenance. They behave differently across platforms, versions, and conditions. Treating them as transparent or frictionless hides the labor and the loss involved in maintaining that smoothness.

Scripts and inscriptions. Every digital tool has a script: embedded assumptions about valid inputs, valid operations, valid outputs. When you use a tool, you partially accept its script. Part of critical technical practice is knowing when you're complying with the script and when you're pushing against it.

What an Impasse Is

An impasse is a moment where the project is telling you something about its own structure. It might feel like a malfunction, but it is carrying information.

Agre calls these hassles: recurring frictions that keep appearing at the same point in your workflow. They are worth writing down and sitting with before you route around them. What they reveal about the gap between your tool's model of the world and the world itself is often more valuable than any fix.

The First Impasse Report asks you to do this formally: describe the problem with enough precision that it becomes a site of inquiry.

For Your Journal — First Impasse Report

Address these four things in your journal entry:

1. Name the impasse.
Describe it precisely. Not "my sensor isn't working" but: what specifically happens, when, under what conditions, and what you expected instead.

2. What assumption did you make that this is disconfirming?
You were assuming something was settled that isn't. What was it? About your tools, your materials, your process, your project?

3. What is it revealing about your matter-network?
Which connection in the network is the site of friction? Is it hardware, software, a protocol, a material behavior, a mismatch between two systems?

4. What becomes possible now that you know this?
An impasse is also a decision point. You can route around it, go through it, or redesign your constraints around it. Which direction does this one want to go?

Failure as Method

Failure and impasse are not obstacles to the project. They are data about the project — revealing the formative conditions, hidden assumptions, and structural tensions that define what the work is actually doing.

Agre's concept of the hassle is foundational here: not a one-time crash, but a recurring friction — something that keeps happening at the same point in your workflow, for reasons you can't quite name. Hassles are worth writing down. They are the system telling you something structural about the gap between how your tool models the world and how the world actually works.

The move Agre names — and this course practices — is to treat impasse as an intellectual resource. When something fails repeatedly, don't just fix it and move on. Ask: what assumption was I making that this failure is disconfirming? What was I treating as settled that isn't? These are the questions the Critical Technical Journal is built to hold.

Manning & Massumi's concept of immanent critique is also relevant here: failure, encountered from inside the practice, reveals the pressure points of the system. You can't see those pressure points from outside. You find them by building, hitting walls, and noticing where the resistance is structural rather than incidental.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9

Key Questions

  • What's a recurring friction in your current workflow — not a one-off problem, but something that keeps coming back? What is that hassle about? What assumption does it keep bumping against?
  • When you hit a dead end in a project, how do you usually respond? Do you route around it, sit with it, or abandon it? What would it look like to study it instead?
  • What's the difference between a failure that tells you something and one that doesn't? How do you tell them apart in the moment?
  • If you had to name your most productive creative failure — the one that most changed how you work — what would you call it? What intermediation term would Agre give it?

Key Readings

  • Agre — *Critical Technical Practice* — the source of the hassle concept and the method of treating recurring friction as intellectual data.
  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — immanent critique as the practice of finding pressure points from inside the system.

Physical-Digital Entanglement

Physical and digital aren't two separate domains with an interface between them. They're entangled — each one shaping the other.

The signal chain at the center of this course — body → sensor → microcontroller → protocol → software → output — is not a pipeline that converts physical reality into digital representation. It's a series of transformations. At each arrow, something is amplified and something is reduced. The body that touches a capacitive sensor is not the same as the threshold-crossing event the sensor registers. The OSC message is not the gesture. Something is always gained; something is always lost. The question is whether you know which — and whether that choice is deliberate.

Magnusson puts it directly: the digital system is "material in its body, electric in function, and symbolic in control." All three layers are active at once. The hardware has physical affordances that shape where you can put it and what it can touch. The firmware has timing properties that shape resolution and latency. The software has representational assumptions that shape what the data means. None of these layers is neutral; together they constitute the instrument.

This thread tracks how physical and digital forces meet, condition each other, and produce something neither could alone — across hardware choices, material selections, installation environments, and bodies moving through space.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 10

Key Questions

  • At each arrow in your signal chain, what's being amplified and what's being reduced? Is that tradeoff intentional?
  • What does your system not know about the body it's sensing — and what can happen in that gap?
  • Where does the physical environment shape the digital behavior, and vice versa? Can you design the entanglement rather than just inheriting it?
  • What material are your sensors touching? What cultural or social charge does that material carry — and does your system acknowledge it?

Key Readings

  • Magnusson — *Epistemic Tools* — on instruments as material-electric-symbolic systems.
  • Camus + Vinck — *Unfolding Digital Materiality* — on the thickness and materiality of digital objects.

Enabling Constraints

A constraint that opens things up rather than just closing them down.

Manning & Massumi's example: gravity. You can't dance without gravity — remove it and there's no jump, no landing, no arc. Gravity is implacable, but it's also what makes the jump possible. Add horizontal momentum and "the arc of the jump will be a collaboration between the action of gravity and the energy and angular momentum of the horizontal movement acting as co-causes." That's an enabling constraint in action — resistance that generates something rather than just blocking it.

The key distinction is between limitative constraints (dead ends) and enabling ones (co-causes). A limitative constraint just says stop. An enabling constraint pushes back in a way that makes something happen that wouldn't otherwise. The trick is figuring out which you're dealing with — and sometimes, how to convert one into the other.

This shows up everywhere in the course. The MPR121 has 12 channels — that's a constraint, but it's also the specific sculptural logic of the thing. OSC over UDP drops packets — that's a constraint, but it forces real-time compositional decisions you wouldn't otherwise have to make. The 3-hour session is a constraint; it's also what allows the kind of depth you can't get in 50 minutes.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 8

Key Questions

  • What's the gravity in your practice — the resistance you can't remove? Is it enabling or just limitative? What would it take to convert it?
  • What's the difference between a constraint someone handed you and one you designed yourself?
  • When you hit a wall, is it a dead end or a co-cause waiting to be found?
  • What if you designed your constraints before you designed the content?

Entanglement

Practitioner, tool, and material do not interact as pre-formed, separate entities. They are mutually constituted through the practice itself. You don't bring a self to a tool — the self that acts is partly made by the act.

Barad's posthumanist performativity replaces interaction (between pre-existing things) with intra-action (the process through which distinct agencies emerge). An apparatus — for Barad, any material-discursive arrangement that makes knowledge possible — doesn't just measure or represent phenomena; it participates in producing them. The researcher, the instrument, and the phenomenon are all co-constituted through the apparatus.

For this course, that means: you are not a stable subject using a neutral tool to study an external object. You are entangled with your hardware, your software, your materials, and the site of your practice. These entanglements are not metaphors — they are structural features of how knowledge gets made. Documenting them honestly is part of what the Critical Technical Journal is for.

Camus & Vinck extend this into digital materiality: the digital is not immaterial. It is made of matter — servers, cables, electricity, screen light. Every "digital" thing has a thickness, a weight, a set of dependencies. The click is a material event. The file is an inscription on physical media. Recognizing this thickness is how you stop treating your tools as transparent windows onto some other reality.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9

Key Questions

  • What are you entangled with in your current project — not just using, but mutually constituted by? What would change about the project if that entanglement changed?
  • Barad replaces "interaction" with "intra-action." What's the difference? Try to locate an intra-action — a moment where the boundaries between you, your tool, and your material were genuinely unclear.
  • Where does the "digital" in your practice have material weight — consume power, require infrastructure, produce heat, leave traces?
  • If your apparatus (the whole material-discursive arrangement of your practice) is not just measuring phenomena but helping produce them — what phenomena is yours producing?

Key Readings

  • Camus + Vinck — *Unfolding Digital Materiality* — on the thickness of digital objects; the materiality of inscription and infrastructure.
  • Manning + Massumi — *Thought in the Act* — the concept of co-causality and conditioning is entanglement in practice.

Research-Creation

Making and thinking as the same act — not phases, not sequence, not illustration.

The standard model: you research something, then you make something that shows what you found. Research-creation refuses that split. Manning & Massumi locate it at what they call the prebifurcation point — the moment before making and thinking get separated into different institutional categories with different evaluation criteria. At that point, making is thinking, and thinking is already a practice.

The thing this rules out is the communication model — the idea that your creative work is "delivering" research results you already had. If the project knows its outcome before it starts, it's not research-creation. The work has to be genuinely open to something it couldn't have predicted.

What that actually requires in practice: conditioning rather than framing. Framing means you set up a structure and the work fills it in. Conditioning means you bring things into contact — materials, tools, questions, bodies, sensors — and stay open to what emerges from their interaction. Neither you nor any individual element fully determines the outcome.

For this course: the journal is not documentation of a separate making process. The making is not an illustration of the thinking. They interpenetrate, or it's not research-creation.

Where This Thread Appears

Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4, Week 5, Week 6, Week 7, Week 8, Week 9, Week 10, Week 11

Key Questions

  • Where in your workflow do making and thinking actually happen at the same time? Can you work from that moment deliberately?
  • Is your project conditioned or framed? What are you holding open, and what are you predetermining?
  • If your practice is already "thinking something" — what is it thinking right now?
  • What in your institution (program, discipline, grading rubric) separates your thinking from your making? How do you work inside that without just reproducing it?

Key Readings

  • Loveless — *Haraway's Dog* — on curiosity as a research method; following a question rather than testing a hypothesis.
